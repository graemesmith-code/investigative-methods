# 09. What did bank surveys show before 2008?

**Question:** Could a cheap, repeatable reading of contemporary banking surveys surface useful warnings? On 30 September 2026, three original-edition US Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) surveys were downloaded and their retail lending tables checked. This tested extraction and arithmetic, not prediction of the financial crisis.

## What the sources contain

The 2004, 2005 and 2006 surveys report examiners' assessments of lending standards. For each product, they give the share of relevant respondents easing, maintaining or tightening standards. Product groups contain different numbers of banks. These are not shares of all lending dollars or forecasts of losses.

## A recipe you can follow

1. Obtain each edition separately and record when it became public.
2. For each lending category, subtract the share tightening standards from the share easing them.
3. Check every category against the page image. Keep missing categories visible and check that percentages add up sensibly.
4. Compare years, then read the regulator's announcement and contemporary reporting.
5. Ask which particular lender, product or promise would need further evidence.

## What happened

The difference between easing and tightening was **0, 17 and 21 percentage points** for residential real estate, and **3, 16 and 32** for conventional home-equity lending. An illustrative ten-point warning rule flagged 0 of 8 categories in 2004, 4 of 8 in 2005 and 4 of 7 in 2006. Raising the threshold to twenty removed every 2005 warning. The threshold was selected after looking at the data.

The regulator was **already publicly warning about weaker standards in July 2005**. Repeating that warning would not itself have been a scoop. A useful next question would connect it to a named lender's growth and assurances about risk.

The extraction also failed initially: ordinary text reading missed six of eight 2005 rows. Checking the expected number of categories exposed the mistake. Reading by the position of cells on the page and checking rendered pages repaired it. The final table holds 160 category/year rows across editions, including repeated historical years—not 160 independent banks.

A second check of mortgage-pricing data showed why a rising headline figure can mislead. Part of the increase reflected interest-rate and reporting mechanics. The Federal Reserve's adjustment needed fields absent from the free public file; the published explanation was read, but that adjustment was not reproduced.

## Cost, limits and the next useful step

No paid data or model calls were used. Human time and platform inference were not costed. There was no AI-versus-human comparison, loss-outcome evaluation or lender-level investigation.

The surveys were retrieved in 2026. Their dates and fingerprints document the copies used, but do not independently prove the exact bytes were online on a particular historical day. Respondents and categories also changed. This famous crisis was selected with hindsight, and a modern model may know its outcome.

The next useful test would choose a group of lenders and an evidence cutoff in advance, preserve ordinary cases, and compare source-supported questions under a fixed reading-time budget.
