# When the records produce a lead but not a story

A public-records screen is a way to decide where to report, not a way to declare what happened. A useful outcome can be a narrower question, a false lead ruled out, or a clear reason to stop.

## Before opening the most interesting files

Write a one-sentence candidate claim. List what would support it, what would contradict it, and what would make it worth publishing even if the narrow allegation fell away. Define the population using a rule you can apply before seeing the outcomes. Retain unmatched records and overlapping controls. If the initial group was selected because anomalies were already visible, call it a pilot, not a representative sample.

## Rebuild the timeline from independent records

- **Keep different dates in different columns.** The date of an event, the effective date of a rule or approval, the date a record was filed, and the date of publication answer different questions. A current dashboard or schedule can show what is listed today without establishing what was true at the time.
- **Separate permission, activity, and consequences.** Read the signed authorization for what was allowed, contemporaneous logs for what was recorded as happening, and independent observations for what followed. A database category is not a legal finding; an operational change after an event is not proof of cause, intent, or a violation.
- **Match records at the finest shared level.** Use exact identifiers, the correct reporting unit, a common clock and time zone, and compatible units or measurement scales. Keep location and time uncertainty explicit. Record which joins used an exact source value and which depended on interpolation or inference.
- **Test an apparent gap with a positive control.** Confirm an archive returns a known record under the same search conditions, then check identifier changes, date bounds, coverage, and reporting lag. Record “not found in this query” or “not accessible” rather than “does not exist.” In a numeric series, keep missing records separate from measured zero.
- **Show how much the conclusion depends on exclusions.** Publish denominators and compare results with and without unusual cases. Do not quietly drop long-running, unresolved, or hard-to-measure events: they may be precisely what the reader needs to understand. An alternate calculation is not, by itself, evidence that either estimate measures the real-world effect.

## Make the stop decision explicit

Before the next round, identify the one record or interview most likely to change the decision. Run a bounded check of prior reporting; ask what the work would add to the published record, not just whether the exact headline has appeared. If the evidence only sharpens an unresolved question and you cannot reach the decisive record, park it rather than accumulating more of the same. Keep the source links, extraction rules, checked claims, counterevidence, and conditions that would justify reopening. Leave a separate reader-friendly explanation rather than making the verification notes carry the narrative.
