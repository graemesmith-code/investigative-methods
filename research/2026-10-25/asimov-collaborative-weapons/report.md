# ASIMOV and the collaborative-weapons pipeline

## A public-source investigation of completion, money, people and what remains worth reporting

**Report prepared for 25 October 2026. Evidence cutoff: 24 October 2026, 21:49 UTC / 23:49 Europe/Rome (CEST).** Financial snapshots and individual retrieval times are recorded separately in the evidence files. No evidence from the following day is implied by the report edition.

This investigation revisits a fixed, previously published procurement screen. It asks two connected but distinct questions: what happened to DARPA's ASIMOV ethical-evaluation programme, and what a group of Air Force collaborative-weapons contracts actually tells us about the emerging kill chain. It does not treat a contract title as a delivered weapon, an unused option as a cancellation, or the absence of a newspaper article containing an award number as proof of a scoop.

## Executive finding

**There is a credible reporting question about ASIMOV's completion and usable outputs. There is not yet evidence of an abrupt, politically motivated shutdown, or of a newly discovered operational missile swarm.** DARPA's page is marked complete, and archived copies bound the appearance of that label between 16 February and 17 June 2026. The original solicitation, however, described a twelve-month base phase followed by an optional twelve-month phase, with possible down-selection. The seven identified awards show $20,495,054 in obligations, not a guaranteed two-year, $39.5 million commitment. Public records do not explain the decision not visibly to exercise the second-stage options. They also do not demonstrate that no other vehicle continued the work.

The strongest question is therefore: **What independently useful ethical-evaluation capability did the public receive from the funded first stage, and why was the programme labelled complete without a visible exercise of the planned second-stage options?** At least three programme- or contract-linked public research outputs exist. Their presence rules out a simplistic “nothing emerged” claim, but does not establish delivery of the integrated, usable evaluation tools envisaged at launch.

The weapons side is a real, funded research pipeline, not just promotional language. The six named cases account for **$12,256,665.44 in net obligations** for collaborative sensing, guidance, coordination and learning research. Yet the current records undermine the proposed picture of “14 active contracts accelerating together.” The reproducible FA8651-prefixed slice contains 22 awards, six with reported current periods extending beyond 24 October 2026. Only two of the six named cases fall into that date group. TALON received more money and a later completion date; EpiSci's exact collaborative-weapons award had its value reduced and end date brought forward. Those are useful differences to investigate, not evidence that every programme advanced or failed in unison.

The shared link is **Eglin-associated Air Force contracting**, not proof that every team worked at the same base. The ASIMOV awards have DARPA funding and Air Force awarding offices. The six named weapons-research awards have Air Force funding; their reported performance locations range across Maryland, Georgia, California, Massachusetts and Florida. Whether AFRL also supplied substantive ASIMOV evaluation staff, data or test infrastructure remains an unanswered institutional question.

**Editorial standard.** Concrete contracts, credible accounts of use and documented tests can support strong practical journalism. Here, the limits on test or platform linkage define the wording of the story; they do not make funded research uninteresting. Mechanism, maturity, comparative advantage and reporting novelty are separate questions; stronger comparative claims need stronger comparative evidence.

## 2. What happened to ASIMOV?

### The original programme was conditional, not a guaranteed two-year commitment

DARPA issued **HR001124S0011 on 25 January 2024**. It sought methods to evaluate how autonomous systems behave in ethically difficult military circumstances. The programme was about measurement and evaluation, not a licence for a machine to decide what is morally permissible, and not itself a weapons-development contract.

The original BAA described a two-phase, twenty-four-month architecture. Its detailed structure was twelve months of Phase I base work, followed by twelve months of Phase II **option** work. It expressly allowed down-selection at the first stage, including fewer performers or a narrower scope. A first-stage final report was already expected around month eleven. This matters: completing base work and not visibly exercising an option is materially different from cancelling an unconditional second year. It still warrants an explanation if the intended longer programme did not proceed. [Original SAM notice](https://sam.gov/opp/225154ac23a246eeaf2a68205441a78f/view); [original BAA PDF](https://sam.gov/api/prod/opps/v3/opportunities/resources/files/698f584a2531423fb4cd344dc81a69aa/download).

DARPA's December 2024 public account described a programme intended to make its work broadly available, including eventual open tools. That creates a legitimate outcomes question. It does not mean every underlying contractor report was promised for immediate unrestricted release; the BAA retained publication controls. [DARPA's launch-stage explanation](https://www.darpa.mil/news/2024/asimov-approaches).

### The completion label has a bounded history, not a known decision date

The [current programme page](https://www.darpa.mil/research/programs/autonomy-standards-and-ideals-with-military-operational-values) says the programme is complete and that the page is retained for reference rather than maintained. The label was absent in the [16 February 2026 archive](https://web.archive.org/web/20260216091212/https://www.darpa.mil/research/programs/autonomy-standards-and-ideals-with-military-operational-values) and present in the [17 June 2026 archive](https://web.archive.org/web/20260617072907/https://www.darpa.mil/research/programs/autonomy-standards-and-ideals-with-military-operational-values). A July sitemap modification date cannot be substituted for this earlier observed appearance. Nor can either web date be assumed to be the date of a programme-management decision.

The visible contract histories contain no option-exercise, termination, cancellation or closeout reason codes. That is a finding about the records located, not a legal conclusion that no such decision occurred. Public reporting lag and the possibility of other award vehicles limit a negative inference. No acceptance report or decision memorandum explaining the completion designation was located in the primary review.

### Results exist; final usable capability remains a separate question

**REBAR**, a Kitware-associated framework, was publicly submitted on 18 May 2026. The paper describes a framework and demonstration, while acknowledging the importance of expert input and simulation choices. A research paper is substantive evidence of work, not an independent acceptance test or proof that an evaluation standard was adopted across the military. The paper acknowledges DARPA; the exact project linkage is reinforced by institutional and partner announcements rather than an exact award number in the paper alone. [REBAR paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.18423).

**MAGNET** appears in a 5 July 2026 chapter, *What Could Possibly Go Wrong? A Hazard-Centric Approach for AI Test and Evaluation*. Its public acknowledgement explicitly identifies FA865125CB002. The public abstract, authors, affiliations and acknowledgement were inspected; the paywalled full chapter was not. That distinction prevents an abstract from becoming a fabricated close reading. The same prime's official subaward descriptions use the name **CALVIN**, expanded as Criteria for AI with Legally Informed Values and Nonmaleficence. The alias is useful for tracing the work, but should not be treated as proof that every MAGNET, CALVIN or ASIMOV component is identical. [Chapter and acknowledgement](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-032-30836-8_46).

**Lockheed Martin's team also published work.** A publicly hosted five-page manuscript, *Towards Reliably Ethical Autonomous Weapon Systems*, names Ronald C. Arkin, Alan R. Wagner, Leete Skinner and Mark Hoffman and explicitly acknowledges FA865125CB018. Skinner and Hoffman are affiliated in the paper with Lockheed Martin ATL. Arkin's university-hosted CV dates it to an August 2025 CASE workshop and lists a related April 2026 presentation on quantifying ethical difficulty. Those are publication-time roles and outputs, not proof of current PI status or a complete software release. [Full primary manuscript, page 1](https://sites.cc.gatech.edu/ai/robot-lab/online-publications/CASEWorkshopFinal.pdf); [Arkin's academic record](https://sites.cc.gatech.edu/aimosaic/faculty/arkin/vita.html).

**Public access remains a narrower unresolved finding.** A bounded check of the seven teams' papers, project pages, public repository metadata and institutional catalogues did not verify a complete, publicly accessible software or dataset release attributable to an ASIMOV contract. This is not proof that no release exists, that accepted deliverables were withheld, or that delivery failed. Some sources were inaccessible; no authenticated notebook, private repository or paid chapter was inspected. Generic repositories with the same acronym, arXiv rendering links and similarly named commercial projects do not establish release of these programme outputs. [Detailed access review](evidence/asimov/software-access-review.md).

**SEED-SET is related, but its attribution remains open.** MIT's 2 April 2026 article and an ICLR paper describe ethics-evaluation research with DARPA support and overlapping MIT/Saab researchers. No ASIMOV or exact-contract acknowledgement was established. Public website/demo assets exist, while the inspected code section promised a later release; a footer led to another project and a notebook route exposed sign-in rather than verified runnable contents. It is meaningful adjacent public work, not yet a confirmed ASIMOV deliverable. [MIT's account](https://news.mit.edu/2026/evaluating-autonomous-systems-ethics-0402); [SEED-SET paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01630).

Publication dates can lag the underlying research; a paper appearing in 2026 does not establish that a second contract phase was exercised or that the experiments occurred then. The accountable question is not “did any paper appear?” It is whether government or independent evaluators can use the resulting tools, what they can reliably measure, what validation was performed, what was accepted, and what was deferred or abandoned.

The six named awards are concrete research purchases. Their technical titles are meaningful evidence of what the government wanted suppliers to explore. They support a story about the direction of development even where a public platform or flight result cannot be linked to the exact order. They do not, by themselves, establish fielding, autonomous lethal authority, or a common integrated system.

The full [six-case narrative](evidence/eglin/portfolio-findings.md) supplies dates, modifications, offices, parent vehicles, pricing types and exact primary links.

Three developments deserve particular attention:

1. **Supersonic collaboration is being bought as research.** APL's nearly $5 million order and GTRI's later order explicitly name supersonic collaborative autonomy. This is a more specific direction than the broad fact that weapons can share information. The open question is what new constraints these projects address, how progress is measured, and whether any public demonstration or follow-on can be tied to them. The absence of a public exact-order test does not erase the funding evidence.
2. **The AI component is not one generic category.** APL's $182,775 order names graph neural networks; EpiSci's award names model-based reinforcement learning; TALON concerns confidence and classification across observations. These are different research functions. Their titles do not establish language models, online self-learning during combat or unconstrained target choice. The journalistic opportunity is to explain what function is being delegated and how it is checked, not to imply that every system is a conversational AI in a missile.
3. **The histories are mixed rather than uniformly accelerating.** TALON's April 2026 action added $1,039,756 and extended its date to September 2027. EpiSci's June 2025 action added $197,297 in obligations while reducing total current/potential value by $806,991 and moving the completion date forward by a year. More money became committed to a smaller award. Neither change, without programme explanation, proves success, failure or speed.

TALON's public SBIR abstract points to an existing RAPTOR autonomy stack and Colosseum-compatible simulation. A maturity claim about the existing stack is not a test result for the new algorithms. The original topic highlights a consequential assurance problem: repeated or dependent observations can create misleading confidence. This is a reporting question about reliability, not evidence that the proposed work solved it. [TALON proposal and named investigator](https://www.sbir.gov/awards/208992).

### What “22” and “six” mean

The starting point is the fixed [384-candidate F2 register](https://github.com/graemesmith-code/investigative-methods/blob/e931fcbc6dc52c00b0565e853637a64fa95773b3/research/2026-10-24/f2-tactical-ai/audit/lane1-tactical-candidates-384.csv). Selecting every identifier beginning FA8651 gives **22 awards: twenty contracts and two grants**. All twenty contract histories were reconciled using 124 published FPDS actions. No discretionary filter was applied to reproduce a supplied count of fourteen.

Six of the twenty-two have current completion dates beyond 24 October 2026. This is a date-field result, not a real-time count of active work. Sixteen have earlier dates, which does not prove legal closeout. The cohort includes peripheral sensors, grants and one ASIMOV award, Saab RESPECT. Saab is **not** among the six later-date entries. It must not be double-counted as direct evidence of weapons development.

The fixed register was a keyword-selected screen, not a census. A separately verified GTRI order, FA865123FA001, funded $2,565,087 of Golden Horde Colosseum hardware-in-the-loop integration but was outside this twenty-two-entry slice. An OKSI award on the same original topic as TALON is another perimeter example; its named hardware must not be transferred to TALON by association. These examples show why neither the prefix nor the selected register defines the whole programme landscape. ## 4. Are the ethics and weapons projects at the same base?

**Same contracting connection: yes. All research at the same base: no. A shared technical programme: not established.**

FA8651 is associated with Eglin's Air Force research acquisition structure. That code identifies a contracting relationship; it is not a location tag for every researcher or a declaration that every award is one programme. Among the six named weapons cases, the reported principal performance places are Laurel, Maryland for both APL orders; Atlanta for GTRI; Poway, California for EpiSci; Woburn, Massachusetts for SSCI; and Eglin itself for Aurora. Across the full twenty-two, three entries report Eglin as the principal performance site. A performance field still does not enumerate all labs, subcontractors or test ranges.

ASIMOV's identified awards have DARPA funding and Air Force awarding offices, with performer locations across several US states and Australia. Its BAA envisaged a government verification-and-validation team spanning relevant organisations. The public evidence reviewed does not establish AFRL Munitions Directorate's exact substantive role in that team. The useful question is whether the office only administered awards or also supplied evaluators, data, facilities or expertise.

There is a genuine professional connection worth asking about: ASIMOV programme manager **Timothy Klausutis** previously held a senior seekers role and was an AFRL Munitions Fellow. That is a documented career history, not proof that he managed these six orders or moved tools between them. His official biography and a September 2026 Ohio State event still identify a DARPA role; a recent departure has not been established. [DARPA biography](https://www.darpa.mil/about/people/timothy-klausutis); [AFRL's 2021 fellowship account](https://www.afrl.af.mil/News/Article/2816035/afrl-scientists-and-engineers-to-be-honored-for-exceptional-career-achievements/); [September 2026 event](https://imr.osu.edu/events/mist-workshop).

## 5. Why does it matter if missiles “talk”?

An ordinary guided weapon can pursue an assigned task using its own sensors and instructions. Networking lets a weapon receive or share information. Collaboration adds coordination based on that information. Autonomy describes functions performed without further human intervention. These ideas overlap but are not synonyms, and none automatically means an AI chooses whom to kill without limits.

The strategic attraction is less dependence on a single remote controller and the possibility of coordinated behaviour when circumstances change. In principle, a group can use information that no member has alone, avoid unnecessary duplication and adapt within its authorised bounds. The public record already includes limited demonstrations of collaboration. What is not established here is how reliably the newer funded approaches work under realistic disruption, or how they alter actual military decisions.

The risks are equally systemic. Shared information can spread a mistake; several observations of the same underlying error can masquerade as corroboration; a simulation can reward behaviour that fails outside its assumptions; and a formal human authorisation at the start may be distant from later machine adjustments. The reporting questions concern the permitted scope of adaptation, independent testing, failure handling, accountability and auditability. They do not require publishing tactical implementation details.

### This history did not begin with the current AI boom

- **2016: operational networked weapons already existed.** The Navy announced initial operational capability for JSOW C-1, including in-flight updates. That is evidence of a controller-to-weapon data link, not proof of autonomous peer collaboration. [NAVAIR, 20 June 2016](https://www.navair.navy.mil/node/23776).
- **2017–2020: Gray Wolf publicly explored networked, affordable cruise-missile concepts.** A 2017 award announcement and 2020 prototype testing are precedents, not evidence that the present orders are direct descendants or fielded systems. [2017 announcement](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/gray-wolf-usaf-awards-lockheed-martin-110-million-for-networked-affordable-cruise-missile-300575550.html); [Air Force's 2020 test account](https://www.aftc.af.mil/News/Article/2273730/416th-flts-afrl-tests-gray-wolf-prototype-cruise-missile/).
- **2020–2021: Golden Horde flew collaborative glide-bomb demonstrations.** The first test communicated but encountered a software-loading problem that prevented the intended subsequent execution. A later six-weapon demonstration met the stated objectives. These were genuine physical demonstrations, not deployment evidence. [First test account, 7 January 2021](https://afresearchlab.com/afrl-demonstrates-first-collaborative-weapon-technologies/); [later demonstration, 27 May 2021](https://afresearchlab.com/afrl-successfully-completes-golden-horde-collaborative-small-diameter-bomb-flight-demonstrations/).
- **2021 onward: Colosseum made common simulation and testing infrastructure part of the story.** APL's competition and GTRI's institutional history explain how suppliers could compare approaches. Competition performance and physical transfer remain different evidentiary stages. [APL, 8 December 2021](https://www.jhuapl.edu/news/news-releases/211208-golden-horde-operation-protovision-first-competition-debuts-new-simulation-environment); [GTRI, 11 November 2021](https://gtri.gatech.edu/newsroom/collaborative-weapons-work-together-respond-changing-conditions).

The broader US effort connects sensors, decision processes, communications and effectors, often described as the kill chain. Replicator, Collaborative Combat Aircraft and CJADC2 are relevant context, but not interchangeable names for these six contracts. GAO has documented coordination and integration problems in CJADC2. That gives a reason to ask whether interfaces, assurance and governance keep pace with individual demonstrations, not to assume this particular portfolio has those exact defects. [GAO, 8 April 2025](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-25-106454).

The published January 2023 autonomy directive required appropriate human judgment and testing safeguards. A **5 June 2026 presidential memorandum** ordered an update within ninety days. The directive version located in the public search and an instruction to update it do not settle whether all internal or subsequently published implementation has been found. Nor does the timing prove why ASIMOV became complete. Policy change, programme management and contractual actions need separate documentary links. [Published directive](https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodd/300009p.pdf); [NSPM-11](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/06/national-security-presidential-memorandum-nspm-11/).

## 7. Prior reporting and the precise novelty question

**The broad subjects have substantial prior coverage.** Rebecca McCarthy's 2 January 2025 New Republic feature, *Inside the Plan to Teach Robots the Laws of War*, is a particularly important challenge to any “unexamined ethics programme” pitch. It contains original interviews and critical discussion of ASIMOV. It precedes the completion label and later results. Patrick Tucker's March 2024 Defense One report also placed ASIMOV in DARPA's research plans. The funding figures in budget requests must not be confused with the obligations audited here. [The New Republic](https://newrepublic.com/article/189236/inside-plan-teach-robots-laws-war); [Defense One](https://www.defenseone.com/technology/2024/03/big-ai-research-darpa-funding-year/394924/).

Golden Horde and networked weapons were also publicly reported years before these orders. An exact identifier absent from a news article does not make the underlying capability new. Conversely, a short contract digest or a launch release does not necessarily exhaust a story about later results, governance or a changed programme trajectory.

The completed Exa audit found **no independent explanation of ASIMOV's completion decision or final integrated benchmark acceptance in the searches conducted**. That supports a focused follow-up, not a proven exclusive scoop. The strongest surviving angle is the disposition, accepted outputs, usable releases and adoption of the funded work. The proposed shutdown-versus-acceleration contrast remains unsupported.

For collaborative weapons, Valerie Insinna's 7 June 2021 Defense News report already covered cooperative-munition tests, an abandoned collaborative-decoy plan and the move toward Colosseum. George Seffers' 1 November 2020 SIGNAL article interviewed a programme manager about CODE's transition and planned Gremlins integration, including developmental limitations. ## 8. Reporting priorities

1. **ASIMOV's decision and public utility.** Obtain the programme-completion rationale, final acceptance/deliverable list, second-stage decision and release locations. Ask whether any work continued elsewhere. The existing evidence supports asking, not alleging suppression or failure.
2. **A precise, results-led account of the collaborative pipeline.** Ask the programme offices and performers which public milestones correspond to the exact supersonic, graph-learning and reinforcement-learning awards. Explain the funded ambition even if details remain restricted, while separating claims of use from claims of performance.
3. **Assurance as an institutional connection.** Establish whether Eglin merely placed the ASIMOV contracts or contributed substantive evaluation, data and personnel. Ask how independent validation is organised across development, testing and procurement. Do not infer a common programme from a prefix.
4. **Explain divergent contract histories.** TALON's extension and EpiSci's shorter, smaller award are concrete changes. Request their public explanations rather than imposing a single acceleration or retrenchment narrative.
5. **Find the people who actually made the decisions.** Named programme managers and investigators are more useful than a generic list of defence-company executives. Verify current roles before outreach; do not manufacture recent departures or assume willingness to speak.

## 9. Methods and limits

This was AI-assisted public-source research and source review. The financial work starts from a fixed published register, selects all FA8651-prefixed entries without discretionary exclusions, retrieves current official award records and reconciles published transaction changes. The ASIMOV review separately identifies seven awards using programme sources, not an assumed consecutive numbering sequence. Data sources, exact URLs, retrieval times, action counts and hashes are retained in the accompanying audit files.

The scope is bounded, not literally every story ever published. Exa is the requested novelty service; ordinary primary-source web retrieval is separately identified and is not relabelled as Exa. Search-result counts are distinguished from deduplicated URLs, relevant sources and actually inspected articles. Inaccessible or partial text is labelled, not treated as a complete review. Syndicated releases and repeated stories are not counted as independent corroboration.

Practical evidence was not rejected merely because controlled academic comparisons were unavailable. No contract value was treated as a prototype's cost or an expenditure total. No missing result was treated as proof of failure. No absence of an exact-PIID article was treated as proof of novelty. Current completion dates were not treated as live activity status, and the ordinary publication lag in DoD procurement data limits claims about recent changes. [GAO's explanation of the reporting delay](https://www.gao.gov/blog/2019/11/13/the-data-act-checking-in-on-spending-transparency).

No outreach, operational weapon-design work or restricted-source acquisition was conducted. This package contains analytical paraphrases, public-source pointers, short necessary excerpts and selected public procurement fields, not full copyrighted article bodies or private administrative records.
