# Why collaborative weapons matter

Missiles exchanging information would matter because it could change how a military uses weapons after launch. A conventional precision weapon may follow an assigned task; a collaborative group could share observations and adjust its collective behaviour within authorised limits. The military’s attraction is adaptability when circumstances change and communications with distant commanders become unreliable. The public-interest question is whether those decisions remain dependable, intelligible and constrained once weapons are airborne. That is a larger challenge than fitting a radio to a missile. [AFRL’s programme overview](https://afresearchlab.com/golden-horde-networked-collaborative-weapons/)

Three ideas need separating. Networking means exchanging information. Collaboration means using it to coordinate actions. Autonomy describes which functions a machine performs without further human intervention. None automatically means a generative-AI model decides whom to kill. Nor is the general ambition new: a 2005 Defense Standardization Program Journal described a long history of communications with in-flight weapons and an emerging push for interoperable networks. The Navy declared its network-enabled JSOW C-1 operational in June 2016, allowing an authorised controller to update or abort a weapon’s mission. That was a fielded networked weapon, not evidence of autonomous weapon-to-weapon teamwork. [DLA journal](https://www.dsp.dla.mil/Portals/26/Documents/Publications/Journal/050701-DSPJ.pdf), [NAVAIR, 20 June 2016](https://www.navair.navy.mil/node/23776)

The most direct rebuttal to a claim that weapons “talking” is new comes from Golden Horde. On 15 December 2020, two modified Small Diameter Bombs established communications in flight. The test also exposed the difference between communicating and working: a software-loading problem prevented the collaborative instructions reaching navigation, and the weapons went to failsafe locations. On 25 May 2021, six bombs released from two F-16s communicated, accepted an external update and completed the final demonstration’s objectives. These were flight demonstrations, not a declaration of operational deployment. [AFRL, 7 January 2021](https://afresearchlab.com/afrl-demonstrates-first-collaborative-weapon-technologies/), [AFRL, 27 May 2021](https://afresearchlab.com/afrl-successfully-completes-golden-horde-collaborative-small-diameter-bomb-flight-demonstrations/)

There were other precedents. Gray Wolf publicly pursued networked cruise-missile collaboration in 2017; the Air Force still described its 2020 testing as proof-of-concept work. DARPA’s CODE demonstrated collaborative unmanned aircraft under degraded communications in 2018–19, using both real aircraft and simulated participants. CODE concerned aircraft, not a deployed missile swarm. These programmes belong in the same history of distributed autonomy, but a shared ambition does not establish a shared codebase or direct technical descent. [Lockheed Martin, 27 December 2017](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/gray-wolf-usaf-awards-lockheed-martin-110-million-for-networked-affordable-cruise-missile-300575550.html), [Air Force, 14 July 2020](https://www.aftc.af.mil/News/Article/2273730/416th-flts-afrl-tests-gray-wolf-prototype-cruise-missile/), [DARPA, 22 March 2019](https://www.darpa.mil/news/2019/code-success)

Golden Horde subsequently shifted towards Colosseum, a testing environment intended to compare technologies and ease their integration. Johns Hopkins APL’s first Operation Protovision competition, scheduled for 8–9 December 2021, put algorithms into a simulated cruise-missile problem. The important industrial idea was to make it easier for different suppliers to compete without each building an entire weapon. A simulation result nevertheless leaves open whether the capability transfers into physical equipment and survives operational testing. [APL, 8 December 2021](https://www.jhuapl.edu/news/news-releases/211208-golden-horde-operation-protovision-first-competition-debuts-new-simulation-environment)

The wider Pentagon landscape has several distinct layers. Combined Joint All-Domain Command and Control, or CJADC2, aims to connect information and decision-making across forces and allies. Collaborative munitions concern what weapons can do with information nearer the point of action. Replicator has funded collaborative-autonomy and command-and-control software, while Collaborative Combat Aircraft pursues uncrewed aircraft working alongside crewed ones. They confront related integration problems; they should not be presented as one programme. In July 2026, the Air Force published common architecture standards intended to reduce proprietary barriers to integrating mission and autonomy software. GAO’s April 2025 CJADC2 audit found projects proceeding largely separately, weak ways to measure collective progress, and unresolved data-sharing barriers. [Air Force, 28 July 2026](https://www.afmc.af.mil/News/Article-Display/Article/4558599/air-force-accelerates-warfighter-tech-releases-foundational-open-architectures/), [GAO](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-25-106454), [DIU, 20 November 2024](https://www.diu.mil/latest/defense-innovation-unit-announces-software-vendors-to-support-replicator), [Air Force, 27 August 2025](https://www.af.mil/News/Article-Display/Article/4288255/collaborative-combat-aircraft-yfq-42a-takes-to-the-air-for-flight-testing/)

Human control must be described precisely. AFRL said Golden Horde would engage only targets or target groups previously chosen by a person and follow preloaded constraints. That does not mean a human individually approved every subsequent adjustment. The published January 2023 Pentagon directive requires “appropriate levels of human judgment over the use of force”, realistic testing and measures against unintended engagements. A June 2026 presidential memorandum ordered an update; the publicly indexed directive still bears the 2023 date. No new directive issuance was verified in this bounded review; inaccessible sources and public indexing limits prevent a claim that no internal or later update exists. [AFRL overview](https://afresearchlab.com/golden-horde-networked-collaborative-weapons/), [directive](https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodd/300009p.pdf), [NSPM-11, 5 June 2026](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/06/national-security-presidential-memorandum-nspm-11/)

The FA8651 awards examined in this investigation therefore need a narrower test of significance than whether missiles can communicate. Do they demonstrate sustained investment, a new integration arrangement, independent assurance, or a funded path into operational inventory? A common contracting prefix alone establishes none of those things. The most consequential finding would identify what changed, who will use it, what testing supports the claim and who remains accountable when software is updated. The remaining obstacles include incompatible systems, unreliable communications, testing beyond rehearsed conditions, and operators’ ability to understand limitations. SIPRI’s 2026 procurement study makes a useful distinction: buying AI responsibly requires an independent ability to test suppliers’ claims, not simply confidence in their demonstrations. [GAO](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-25-106454), [SIPRI, February 2026](https://www.sipri.org/publications/2026/other-publications/responsible-procurement-military-artificial-intelligence)
