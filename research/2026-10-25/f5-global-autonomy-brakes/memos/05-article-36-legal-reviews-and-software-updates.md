# F5 / 05 — Legal weapons reviews and continuous software change
## The rule is not “review once”; investigate who classifies the change

**Research cutoff:** 25 October 2026. **Status:** integrated from both reporting lanes after their documented editor rounds; pending final global editorial review; not a legal opinion or finding of misconduct. **Authoring lane:** legal weapons review, distinct from safety, airworthiness, cybersecurity authorization, acquisition approval and ethical assurance. All source access was public/free; no outreach, records request, payment or external publication was made. Companion files contain the comparison and interview plan.

### Editorial bottom line

The proposed “traditional missile reviewed once, AI updates escape review” framing is not supported as a general rule. The U.S. Army expressly addresses changed fielded systems and rapid acquisition. Britain describes iterative review and material-change reassessment. Australia defines material modification by altered function, intended use or effects. Those are reasons to investigate **implementation and change classification**, not to declare an existing universal exemption. [Army AR 27–53](https://armypubs.army.mil/epubs/DR_pubs/DR_a/pdf/web/ARN8435_AR27-53_Final_Web.pdf), [UK response, February 2024, paragraphs 24–25](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/65cb77caa7ded0000c79e526/Government_response_to_the_House_of_Lords_AI_in_Weapon_Systems_Committee_Report.pdf), [Australian guide, 10 April 2024, Part I](https://legalreviewportal.org/files/au/2024-04-10-adf_guide_to_art_36_legal_review.pdf).

Three defensible reporting leads emerge:

1. **A fresh Dutch document chain:** the October 2026 disclosure shows legal conditions carried into actual June 2024 SMASH use permission. The strongest benign interpretation is that restrictions carried through as intended. A 2026 answer promises reassessment of new versions; the missing link is a version-specific decision and field-compliance evidence, not proof of a bypass.
2. **A contemporary warning by identifiable Army lawyers:** a centralized re-review process risks being too slow for battlefield adaptation. Their published example was a workshop scenario, not a disclosed operational bypass.
3. **A live U.S. accountability question:** a June 2026 presidential memorandum ordered a new autonomy directive within 90 days. The official public register still indexed January 2023 when searched; an issued replacement was not verified. Congress has distinct hooks for directive modifications, individual waivers and annual approval/review reporting.

No dated public record located in this pass establishes that DAWG, Agincourt, or a specific transfer to Ukraine or the Middle East used an AI update to bypass a required legal review. Nonpublication is not proof of non-review.

## 2. What must not be conflated

### Treaty duty versus U.S. policy

Article 36 of Additional Protocol I requires a party to determine the legality of a new weapon, means or method during study, development, acquisition or adoption. It does not prescribe one universal national committee, software threshold, or public register. The United States signed but did not ratify Protocol I; Britain, Australia, Canada, New Zealand, France, the Netherlands and Sweden are parties. U.S. weapons-review requirements therefore should not be described as U.S. compliance with an Article 36 treaty obligation. Nor does nonparty status remove other applicable law-of-war duties. [ICRC treaty and party record](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/ihl-treaties/api-1977/state-parties), [ICRC Article 36 text](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/ihl-treaties/api-1977/article-36).

The ICRC's 2006 review guide treats changes to existing weapons and new evidence about performance as possible reasons to revisit legality. It is guidance, not an amendment to the treaty or proof of national practice. [ICRC guide](https://international-review.icrc.org/sites/default/files/irrc_864_11.pdf).

### Five different decisions

For any alleged “approval,” establish whether the document is:

- a legal opinion on a weapon/system and its ordinary or intended use;
- special senior policy approval for autonomous functions;
- a safety/airworthiness/cybersecurity approval;
- acquisition or fielding authorization; or
- a commander's assessment of a particular use of force.

A legal weapon can be used unlawfully; a lawful proposed use does not prove a software release passed its technical assurance gate. A vendor's statement that a system is “approved” cannot establish all five. This is the analytical framework of this memo, not a claim that every jurisdiction organizes the decisions identically.

### Three technically different kinds of “update”

**Offline model replacement:** a new trained model is packaged and released as a version. The legal question is what changes relative to the previously reviewed system and use—not merely whether weights changed.

**Online/in-situ learning:** the deployed system changes through experience. A static version number may not adequately describe the reviewed behaviour. Scholarship identifies the problem of defining permissible learning and the evidential limits of reviewing an evolving capability; periodic reviews alone do not establish that all intervening behaviour was lawful. [Tim McFarland and Zena Assaad, 10 February 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10676-023-09688-9).

**Changed use without changed software:** another mission, environment, target category, integration or human-supervision arrangement can matter even when the executable is unchanged. Conversely, a minor update does not necessarily invalidate every prior finding. “Over the air” describes delivery, not a legal exemption. The actual threshold must come from the jurisdiction's instrument and the scope of the earlier decision.

## 3. United States: multiple gates, not a one-time exemption

### Ordinary Army review

AR 27–53, dated 23 September 2019, requires early/follow-on review and a final review before initial production. Paragraph 6f invalidates the original opinion when a fielded system changes sufficiently, including substantial intended-use or anticipated-effects changes. Paragraphs 6g–i expressly address experiments, rapid fielding and urgent need: speed affects processing, not the legal conclusion. Paragraph 6k designates TJAG's National Security Law Division and permits other specific-system designees. Paragraph 9 prohibits relevant activity without requisite review. These are rule-text findings, not measured compliance. [AR 27–53, paragraphs 6–9](https://armypubs.army.mil/epubs/DR_pubs/DR_a/pdf/web/ARN8435_AR27-53_Final_Web.pdf).

**Verification lead, not an established current comparison:** official indexed extracts of the 2018 Air Force instruction refer to modifications and previously unanticipated uses. The full relevant document and current supersession status were not inspected successfully; do not rely on its fine-grained assignments without that check. [AFI 51–401, 3 August 2018, paragraphs 2.5–2.6 and 6–7](https://static.e-publishing.af.mil/production/1/af_ja/publication/afi51-401/afi51-401.pdf).

### Special autonomous-weapons review

The January 2023 DoDD 3000.09 provides an additional senior gate for covered autonomous systems. Changes in algorithms, mission, environment, targets or expected adversarial countermeasures substantially outside an earlier approval can require renewed senior review. Some categories are exempt from that **special gate**, not from ordinary legal review. Section 4.2 permits Deputy Secretary waivers for urgent military need of specified directive requirements; it is not a general authorization to disregard the law of war. Section 3 separately addresses iterative testing and subsequent changes. [DoDD 3000.09, 25 January 2023, sections 1.2, 3–4](https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodd/300009p.PDF?ver=e0YrG458bVDl3-oyAOJjOw%3D%3D).

**Retrieval/provenance:** official PDF downloads returned HTTP 403. The complete 24-page January 2023 directive was subsequently inspected in a [public reproduction](https://esut.de/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/300009p.pdf), with relevant sections matching the indexed official text. This resolves the passage-access gap, not the question of the operative 2026 instrument. The public issuances register indexed 25 January 2023, with no listed change, when searched on the cutoff date. This is not proof no nonpublic or newly issued replacement exists. [Official directives register](https://www.esd.whs.mil/Directives/issuances/dodd/).

### Four distinct accountability hooks, plus a named oversight inquiry

President Donald Trump's NSPM-11, signed 5 June 2026, ordered a 3000.09 update within 90 days and annual review thereafter. Ninety calendar days leads to 3 September 2026; the order itself does not prove delivery. It also addresses unauthorized vendor/adversary disabling or material modification and requires assurance. That vendor-control provision should not be equated with removing military human controls. [NSPM-11, sections 2(c), 3(a)](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/06/national-security-presidential-memorandum-nspm-11/).

10 U.S.C. §130g, added by Public Law 119–60 on 18 December 2025, requires written notice to congressional defense committees within 30 days of a 3000.09/successor waiver. Notice must identify rationale, covered system/technology and expected duration; it is unclassified but may include a classified annex. This is an unusually precise records lead. It does not show that a waiver was issued or require public release of every notification. [Official U.S. Code text](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section130g-1).

Two further enacted provisions must be kept distinct from that individual-waiver notice:

- **Policy-change briefing:** FY2024 NDAA §251 requires the Secretary to brief congressional defense committees within 30 days after modifying 3000.09, describing the change and reasons. [Public Law 118–31, §251](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-118publ31/html/PLAW-118publ31.htm).
- **Annual review/approval report:** FY2025 NDAA §1066 requires a first report by 31 December 2025 and annual reporting until termination on 31 December 2029, covering approved systems/dates, waivers/dates, pending reviews and nonapprovals. Reports are unclassified but may contain classified annexes. [Public Law 118–159, §1066, 138 Stat. 2065–66](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-118publ159/pdf/PLAW-118publ159.pdf). FY2026 NDAA §1061(b) adds legal reviews to waiver, pending and nonapproval categories. The enrolled text and official enactment history verify the amendment; reporting language does not itself grant legal-review waiver power. [Enrolled S.1071, §1061](https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s1071/BILLS-119s1071enr.htm), [official enactment account, page III](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CPRT-119HPRT62660/pdf/CPRT-119HPRT62660.pdf).

These create specific records leads, not evidence that a modification, waiver or report occurred or that Congress received it. Ask for receipt dates, transmitting offices, reporting-period scope and releasable portions.

Senator Ruben Gallego's letter dated **12 June 2026**, seeking a reply by 26 June, asks Hegseth six questions about the revision, troop/civilian safeguards and allied consultation—including whether DAWG has dedicated civilian-harm-prevention staff. It copies Emil Michael, identified as USD(R&E), and Lt. Gen. Stephen M. Marks, identified as DAWG director, on that date. Those are accountable interview leads, not findings that safeguards were removed. No published reply was located in targeted searches of Gallego's and the department's public sites by this cutoff; private receipt/nonreceipt remains unknown. [Original two-page letter](https://www.gallego.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Final_Gallego-Letter_Directive-3000.09.pdf).

### Contemporary friction: attributable, but not a disclosed bypass

Colonel Andrew McKee and Lieutenant Colonel Christina Colclough wrote on 12 November 2025 that centralized Army review risks failing to keep pace with tactical adaptation. They proposed decentralization, theater-level authority and faster processing. Their bios then identified McKee as U.S. Army Europe-Africa's judge advocate and Colclough as a West Point assistant professor. They expressly wrote in personal capacities.

The software-change example came from a May 2025 symposium exercise. References to Ukraine explain urgency but do not establish that a named Ukrainian or U.S. model release was delayed, waived or fielded without review. This is a strong interview lead about a perceived institutional bottleneck, not an empirical queue-time study. [McKee and Colclough](https://lieber.westpoint.edu/legal-reviews-autonomous-weapons-tactical-edge/).

Michael W. Meier's 27 August 2025 essay independently argues that drone adaptation demands a better review process while distinguishing changes that warrant renewed review from those that do not. Treat that as expert argument, not an instruction, audit or operational case. [Meier](https://lieber.westpoint.edu/unleashing-drone-dominance-rethinking-department-defense-weapons-reviews/).

## 4. Allies: explicit re-review rules, uneven public implementation evidence

### Britain

In February 2024 the government rejected a simple one-off-release conception and said a material change caused by an update would appropriately prompt re-review. It acknowledged that learning systems challenge existing procedures. This responded to a Lords request for demonstrable thresholds for systems learning after deployment; a broad materiality statement is not itself a disclosed quantitative threshold, decision log or worked case. [Government response, paragraphs 24–25 and recommendation 22](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/65cb77caa7ded0000c79e526/Government_response_to_the_House_of_Lords_AI_in_Weapon_Systems_Committee_Report.pdf).

The UK's 2024 voluntary IHL report identifies DCDC tri-service legal officers and several acquisition milestones. Urgent Operational Requirements are not exempt: an oral review may precede service entry, with a comprehensive written review following. A foreign state's prior review does not replace the UK's own. [UK voluntary report, section 3](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/implementation-of-international-humanitarian-law-at-domestic-level-2024-voluntary-report/voluntary-report-on-the-implementation-of-international-humanitarian-law-at-domestic-level-second-edition).

**Do not mislabel a real adjacent case.** Shield AI's parliamentary submission dated 27 July 2026 reports uncertainty over V-BAT's UK aviation authority, a duty-holder workaround and burdensome reapproval following updates informed by Ukraine. It is vendor testimony about aviation assurance, not a disclosed Article 36 dispute. Ask MOD for its account and the actual decisions. [DEF0035, paragraphs 77–82](https://committees.parliament.uk/writtenevidence/168308/html/).

### Australia

The guide signed by Colonel Damian Copeland, Director of Operations and International Law, on 10 April 2024 assigns threshold decisions to DOIL and requires requestors to consult it on material modifications. Part III adds a functional assessment for AI/autonomy. The final review remains valid only within the capability/use assessed. The guide's detail is evidence of a process, not proof that every deployed update followed it. [Australian guide, Part I paragraphs 7–8, 15; Part III](https://legalreviewportal.org/files/au/2024-04-10-adf_guide_to_art_36_legal_review.pdf).

Copeland's 2026 Army Research Centre essay examines the integration of legal review throughout military AI assurance, including functions outside a narrow weapon label. It is useful to test the boundary between a weapons opinion and continuing governance; do not present its recommendations as an adopted amendment. [Army Research Centre essay](https://researchcentre.army.gov.au/library/collections/safety-legal-and-ethical-implications-artificial-intelligence-military-domain/role-article-36-legal-reviews-across-military-ai-assurance-lifecycle).

### Canada and New Zealand: keep the urgent exceptions narrow

Canada's soldier handbook, page dated 2 September 2025, permits commanders, subject to orders and urgent battlefield circumstances, temporarily to authorize captured, improvised or altered weapons without a formal legal review when satisfied the use complies with LOAC. It normally calls for legal-adviser support. This is an actual published procedural exception, not an AI-specific exemption or permission for unlawful weapons. Its footnote cites the DM/CDS Article 36 directive, whose full text was not located. [Handbook, chapter 6, paragraph 9](https://www.canada.ca/en/department-national-defence/corporate/policies-standards/legal-policies-directives/soldier-handbook-law-armed-conflict-land-warfare/ch-6-weapons-ammunition.html).

A Canadian disclosure index records a similarly titled directive received for signature on 24 September 2024, reference SJS2024-0002884. Metadata does not establish signature, contents or commencement. A legal-review portal reports a July 2025 modernization; absent primary text, that date and its change rules remain a lead, not a verified operative instrument. [Official metadata](https://search.open.canada.ca/briefing_titles/record/dnd-mdn%2CSJS2024-0002884), [portal entry, secondary](https://legalreviewportal.org/states/canada).

New Zealand's publicly hosted DM 69, second edition, volume 4, requires sponsors to approach the Director of Defence Legal Services early, furnish intended use/effects and consider likely environments; results go to the Chief of Defence Force and sponsor. Foreign reviews are informative, not dispositive. An emergency provision for captured weapons is separate. No explicit model-update threshold was identified in the reviewed chapter. The online copy is marked uncontrolled; verify the current amendment before treating it as an exhaustive 2026 procedure. [DM 69, paragraphs 7.3.3 and 7.4.1–7.4.6](https://nzdf.mil.nz/assets/Uploads/DocumentLibrary/DM-69-2ed-vol4.pdf).

### France and Sweden

France's 2022 military manual describes review during procurement and use, including legally consequential project changes, new functions and evolving doctrine. EMA and DGA perform initial screening; relevant legal review involves DAJ. Operational urgency and off-the-shelf acquisition remain within scope. The primary manual was retrieved through a specialist transcription; the government PDF timed out. Confirm against the official edition and any later instruction. [Manual, section 5.6, primary text reproduced by legal-review portal](https://legalreviewportal.org/positions/v2adc).

Swedish ordinance SFS 2007:936 requires agencies to notify the weapons-project delegation of study, development, acquisition and modification projects promptly; the delegation also follows important reviewed projects. Its decisions can call for changes or operational restrictions. FOI's November 2025 guide candidly notes the ordinance's brevity and interpretive uncertainty, recommends referral when uncertain, and places initial notification routines with submitting agencies. The guide is official research guidance, not a new ordinance. [Ordinance, sections 2, 7, 13](https://rkrattsbaser.gov.se/sfst?bet=2007%3A936), [FOI Memo 8995, Pontus Winther and Miriam Ingeson](https://www.foi.se/rest-api/report/FOI%20Memo%208995).

## 5. Test/configuration baselines: investigate the connection, not just the paperwork

The September 2025 U.S. Developmental Test and Evaluation of Autonomous Systems guidebook discusses dynamic learning, continuing evaluation and post-acceptance activity. It supports asking how changing software is tested throughout its life. It does not itself supply a legal opinion on any named release. [OUSD(R&E) guidebook, sections 4.2.11, 5.2.5, 5.3.6](https://www.cto.mil/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/DTE-of-AS-GB.pdf).

The decisive investigative comparison is between:
1. what the legal reviewer was told and permitted;
2. the system/model/configuration actually released;
3. the use and environment actually authorized; and
4. the change decision assigning or declining re-review.

A release identifier, model/data lineage, test package reference, materiality decision and approval date can show the chain without publishing sensitive technical data. Ask for redacted metadata and non-operational summaries first. Do not publish targeting parameters, vulnerabilities or instructions for getting an update past a gate.

The 26 May 2023 AUKUS announcement confirms live model retraining in flight and model interchange among national platforms during a representative ISR trial. It establishes technical activity, **not** combat deployment, an autonomous weapon release, or absence of legal review. [UK trial announcement](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/world-first-as-uk-hosts-inaugural-aukus-ai-and-autonomy-trial).

An official Army account dated 15 October 2026 confirms Hegseth's Agincourt initiative and identifies Owen West as head of the Direct Reporting Portfolio Manager for Unmanned Systems. It documents emphasis on adaptation and fielding, not a waiver of weapons-law review. **Negative finding, bounded to this search:** no identified primary record pairs a particular DAWG/Agincourt or Ukraine/Middle East software release with a required legal gate, a dated refusal/waiver/non-referral, and a subsequent fielding outcome. A claim of bypass needs that chain; classification, a missing public opinion, or an official declining comment cannot substitute for it.

## 7. What would make this publishable as an investigation

The established finding is narrower and more useful: **re-review duties exist, and the Dutch record shows legal conditions carrying into actual permission.** The investigable question is whether institutions can reliably classify and trace later changes. The hypothesis that adaptation outruns review remains unproved; the same evidence can be consistent with safeguards working. The decisive evidence must link baseline/change, applicable trigger, missing required decision and release/use outcome—not merely a fast update or an unpublished opinion. It may combine credible attributable firsthand accounts and documentary corroboration; do not demand an unattainable complete internal file as the only possible proof.

Priority evidence requests to prepare—not filed here:

- A current signed directive, amendment history and delegation instrument.
- Redacted review register entries linking an original opinion and later changes.
- Materiality determinations, including documented decisions that no new legal review was needed.
- Date-stamped referrals, information requests, opinions and release approvals, distinguishing reviewer time from missing technical evidence.
- Conditions of use and records showing who translated them into orders, training and release controls.
- Actual urgent exemptions/waivers, duration, renewal and post-use review; relevant congressional notification metadata.
- One named program's baseline and change history, with operational details removed.
- For transfers, the contractual/official responsibility matrix for recipient updates and notice to the donor.

A lawyer's role is not interchangeable with the person accepting operational risk. Interview each office separately, give the department and manufacturer the exact documentary discrepancy, and offer a response before characterizing delay, evasion or failure.

## 9. Additional corroboration and scope checks from the second reporting lane

The [DoDD 2311.01 Law of War Program, 2 July 2020](https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodd/231101p.pdf), paragraph 2.6(e), independently assigns components responsibility for review of intended acquisition, procurement or modification. The January 2023 autonomy directive repeats that legal-review duty in paragraph 2.9(c). These are additional rule-text anchors, not evidence that an individual release was reviewed.

NSPM-11 section 3(f) expressly rescinds and replaces NSM-25 and associated guidance. Earlier NSM-25 arrangements must not be carried into a description of current policy without qualification. The parent team's separately verified [GAO-27-107837, 14 October 2026](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-27-107837), records acceleration policy and Responsible AI implementation challenges. Organizational capacity is relevant context, but does not prove a weapons-law review was skipped.

The [Canadian government's 2024 voluntary IHL implementation report](https://cdn.redcross.ca/cnchl/2024-canada-voluntary-report-on-the-domestic-implementation-of-ihl-final.pdf), page 30, describes OJAG opinions on new or modified capabilities and early acquisition referrals; its [2024 UN submission](https://docs-library.unoda.org/General_Assembly_First_Committee_-Seventy-Ninth_session_(2024)/78-241-Canada-EN.pdf) also describes national review and continuing IHL compliance. These are government process accounts, not independent compliance audits. They do not resolve the current directive-text gap described above.

[Tobias Vestner and Nicolò Borgesano, 12 December 2025](https://lieber.westpoint.edu/legal-reviews-war-algorithms-cyber-weapons-ai-systems/), use cyber review practice to examine continuing AI review and significant-versus-minor changes. This is a useful analogy, not a universally binding AI update rule. [Damian Copeland, 4 March 2024](https://lieber.westpoint.edu/aws-functional-approach/), proposes reviewing legally regulated functions, operational context and human control across the lifecycle; the proposal is distinct from adopted national instruments.

In [written evidence dated 22 June 2026](https://committees.parliament.uk/writtenevidence/167677/html/), Aurel Sari argues that private dual-use development can accumulate design commitments before government legal engagement, increasing redesign risk. His argument that some decision-support services fall outside Article 36 despite LOAC consequences is expert evidence, not a parliamentary finding or a universal classification of all AI decision support. National definitions and the actual function matter.

The older [UK Weapon Reviews explanation, published 8 March 2016](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a80bf5f40f0b62305b8cec5/20160308-UK_weapon_reviews.pdf), describes development-stage legal engagement, further review when use/capability changes, and a two-lawyer approval practice. It is historical corroboration, not proof of the current staffing arrangement or an individual update review. Its public PDF retains restrictive legacy release wording; this package links and analyzes it without rehosting its text or PDF. The later government accounts above are the principal current process sources.

The [UK's 20 February 2026 regulatory-review call](https://www.gov.uk/government/calls-for-evidence/review-of-mod-legislation-governing-autonomous-systems/review-of-the-regulatory-system-governing-uncrewed-and-autonomous-systems-in-defence), closed 13 March, requests evidence of time/cost and testing/deployment burdens. This corroborates official recognition of friction; it does not establish subsequent removal of legal safeguards. Aviation assurance reapproval, technical release, ethical assurance and Article 36 review remain distinct even when triggered by the same software change.

The US is not an AP I party, and the existence or extent of a general customary duty to review is debated. That debate must not obscure explicit domestic review rules or imply other applicable law-of-war duties vanish. Conversely, engineering reliability alone cannot settle all legal questions: it can provide crucial evidence about effects and predictability, while context, intended use and human responsibility remain material.
