# Legal review when software keeps changing

Research cutoff: 25 October 2026. The premise that a traditional weapon receives one immutable legal sign-off is too simple. Public US and allied procedures already anticipate modifications. The stronger investigation concerns the change threshold, evidence available to reviewers, version control and speed of reassessment. No public record examined here proves that DAWG, Project Agincourt or a named Ukraine/Middle East transfer bypassed a required legal review.

## Treaty obligation and US policy are different legal routes

[Article 36 of Additional Protocol I](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/ihl-treaties/api-1977/article-36) requires a state party to assess whether a new weapon, means or method of warfare would be prohibited in some or all circumstances by applicable international law. It does not prescribe a single international certification form or a universal source-code inspection. The [ICRC treaty record for the United States](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/ihl-treaties/treaties-and-states-parties?order=ASC&sort=state&state=US) shows signature of AP I in 1977, not ratification. Do not report US weapons reviews as an Article 36 treaty obligation. Whether a more general customary review duty exists is disputed in legal scholarship; domestic US review policy is explicit regardless.

The [ICRC's 2006 legal-review guide](https://www.icrc.org/en/publication/0902-guide-legal-review-new-weapons-means-and-methods-warfare-measures-implement-article) treats review as a multidisciplinary mechanism and addresses changed weapons as well as new ones. Its framework is neither proof that every state implements it nor permission to assume that technical reliability settles the whole legal question. A safe platform can still be used unlawfully; evidence about predictable effects can nevertheless be essential to determining lawful use.

## The US modification gate exists on paper

[DoDD 2311.01, 2 July 2020](https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodd/231101p.pdf), paragraph 2.6(e), assigns components responsibility for law-of-war review of intended acquisition, procurement or modification of weapons. [DoDD 3000.09, 25 January 2023](https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodd/300009p.pdf), paragraph 2.9(c), repeats the modification-review requirement specifically for autonomous and semi-autonomous weapons. Its senior autonomy approval process is supplementary, with scope exceptions; an exception from that senior process is not automatically an exception from legal review. Its design/environment monitoring provisions also anticipate additional testing when relevant conditions change. These policies establish duties and process, not proof of completed reviews for any named system.

[Army Regulation 27–53, 23 September 2019](https://armypubs.army.mil/epubs/DR_pubs/DR_a/pdf/web/ARN8435_AR27-53_Final_Web.pdf) is particularly concrete. Paragraph 6(f) invalidates an opinion when a fielded weapon is no longer the same system or capability described to the reviewer, including substantial changes in intended use or anticipated effects. Paragraphs 6(g)–(i) cover preliminary review of operationally intended experiments and rapid fielding, substantial enhancements including changed effects or accuracy, and rapid acquisition. Urgency affects review speed, not the legality determination. Paragraph 7 permits expedited review; paragraph 9 prohibits covered acquisition/fielding/employment without the required review. These provisions are evidence against a categorical rapid-fielding loophole, while leaving compliance in particular cases unverified.

For a model update, the practical question is therefore whether changed weights, data, interfaces, integration or operating conditions alter the previously reviewed capability, effects or use. File size, update cadence and the label “software patch” do not alone settle that question. Nor does every retraining necessarily require a complete new review: the significance of the change must be assessed under the governing national process. Public sources do not yield a universal numerical threshold.

## The currentness trap: a revision order is not a published replacement

The primary [White House NSPM-11, 5 June 2026](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/06/national-security-presidential-memorandum-nspm-11/), section 3(a), orders an update to DoDD 3000.09 within 90 days and annual review thereafter. Section 3(f) rescinds and replaces NSM-25 and associated guidance. Section 2 couples accelerated adoption with assurance, government authority over material modification, security and functional testing. This is direct evidence of policy pressure and changed governance context. It is not evidence that required legal reviews have been abolished or that a new autonomy directive was issued on time.

Public ESD and official-host searches in this pass returned the January 2023 directive. No published replacement text was verified. Accordingly this memo identifies exactly which published version it analyzes and leaves the status of any later implementation unresolved. It does not treat NSM-25's former framework as current. The parent team's retrieved [GAO report GAO-27-107837, 14 October 2026](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-27-107837), independently records January 2026 acceleration policy and Responsible AI implementation challenges. That oversight evidence concerns organizational capacity; it does not by itself prove a particular weapons-review violation.

## Allies: materially changed capability is already a recognized trigger

The [UK MOD's public explanation, UK Weapon Reviews, 8 March 2016](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a80bf5f40f0b62305b8cec5/20160308-UK_weapon_reviews.pdf), says changed system use or capability receives further review and describes lawyer involvement during development and peer review. This is a published process statement, not a current list of model-version approvals. The source has restrictive legacy release language despite its public host: this package links and analyzes it, without rehosting the text or PDF.

[Australia's Guide to the Legal Review of New Weapons, Means or Methods of Warfare, 10 April 2024](https://legalreviewportal.org/files/au/2024-04-10-adf_guide_to_art_36_legal_review.pdf), is a government-authored document publicly mirrored by APILS. Part I identifies a before-service requirement for new or materially modified capabilities. Part III adds a functional review for AI/autonomous capabilities. It asks reviewers to connect legally regulated functions to technical/operational evidence and contextual human control. The guide distinguishes legally required reviews from some reviews recommended as policy, including certain transfers. It does not establish that every overseas transfer has received a review or that an exporting state's review substitutes for the acquirer's responsibilities.

The [Government of Canada's 2024 voluntary IHL implementation report](https://cdn.redcross.ca/cnchl/2024-canada-voluntary-report-on-the-domestic-implementation-of-ihl-final.pdf), page 30, says OJAG assesses new or modified capabilities and describes early submission in acquisition. Its [2024 UN submission](https://docs-library.unoda.org/General_Assembly_First_Committee_-Seventy-Ninth_session_(2024)/78-241-Canada-EN.pdf) also describes national review and continuing IHL compliance. These are public government accounts of process. The APILS Canada country page references a July 2025 directive, but its full primary text was not retrieved here; it is a follow-up lead rather than a basis for silently updating the reported rule.

## A public case where reviewers imposed conditions: Dutch SMASH

The Dutch [Opinion 18 on SMASH 2000, published 13 August 2021](https://www.rijksoverheid.nl/documenten/2021/08/13/advies-18-gebruik-smash-2000-smart-shooter), supplies a concrete historical counterexample to rule-only reporting. Its [four-page original](https://open.overheid.nl/documenten/ronl-ef30b0d7-827d-4805-a84e-ad0ccb4bd890/pdf) permits Dutch military use subject to training, use only against UAVs, and circumstances with limited risk of an incorrect target lock. Other purposes were prohibited pending further testing and evaluation of mitigation. The reviewers identified a risk that misplaced confidence and incorrect locks in complex situations could increase unintended harm. The review links observed performance limitations to conditions of permitted use; it does not guarantee effective compliance in operations. The 2021 publication is outside the requested 2023–2026 legal-reporting window and is retained as a historical case, not silently counted as a new review.

There is a current follow-up: in [official parliamentary answers published 9 February 2026](https://zoek.officielebekendmakingen.nl/ah-tk-20252026-1049.html), answer 16 calls the 2021 opinion binding, distinguishes the aiming aid from an independent weapon, and states that new versions will be reassessed for conditions of Dutch military use. This verifies that government publicly invoked the review and version reassessment in 2026. It does not establish that the 2021 conditions apply unchanged to SMASH 3000, that a later review has been completed, or that the restrictions were always obeyed. No use against people, procurement total, or unlawful deployment is inferred. The valuable reporting question is the published connection between reviewed version, operational limitation and subsequent version reassessment.

## What military-law practitioners actually identify as friction

[Andrew McKee and Christina Colclough, Lieber Institute, 12 November 2025](https://lieber.westpoint.edu/legal-reviews-autonomous-weapons-tactical-edge/), analyze a symposium scenario in which enemy deception prompts rapid changes to a recognition model. They warn that centralized reassessment can lag tactical adaptation and propose initial review closer to units, with escalation and prioritization. Their point is process reform, not an account of a documented unlawful bypass. The article's recommendations are the authors' views, not binding Army policy.

[Tobias Vestner and Nicolò Borgesano, 12 December 2025](https://lieber.westpoint.edu/legal-reviews-war-algorithms-cyber-weapons-ai-systems/), use cyber review practice to explore continuing AI review, distinguishing significant changes from minor changes without operational effects. That analogy is useful, but is not itself a universally binding AI update rule. [Damian Copeland, 4 March 2024](https://lieber.westpoint.edu/aws-functional-approach/), argues for reviewing legally significant functions across the lifecycle and their operational context. This approach connects reliability, control and law without pretending that an engineering benchmark alone constitutes a legal decision.

A separate 22 June 2026 [Aurel Sari submission to the UK Parliament](https://committees.parliament.uk/writtenevidence/167677/html/) identifies a supplier-development problem: privately developed dual-use AI may accumulate design commitments before government legal engagement. It also argues that some decision support falls outside Article 36's weapon/means/method scope despite substantial LOAC consequences. That is evidence of an expert's stated concern, not a parliamentary finding about every AI decision-support system. The scope boundary is contested and national definitions matter.

The [UK's February 2026 public regulatory-review call](https://www.gov.uk/government/calls-for-evidence/review-of-mod-legislation-governing-autonomous-systems/review-of-the-regulatory-system-governing-uncrewed-and-autonomous-systems-in-defence) is a current primary acknowledgement that autonomy testing and deployment face regulatory friction. The closed call is evidence of a review process; it is not evidence that safeguards were subsequently removed.

## A reporting test that can distinguish a real gap from a slogan

For a named deployment, the informative evidence would be the reviewed version and authorized use; the update/change assessment; changed operational performance and legal assumptions; the responsible review authority; any approved restrictions and reassessment; and the actual deployed version. The absence of a public legal opinion is not evidence that no opinion exists. A paper policy also cannot prove that version changes were checked in operations.

For DAWG, Agincourt, or transferred systems, establish the precise national authority and capability first. A budget announcement, rapid prototype contract or capability demonstration is not a finding that a legal gate was bypassed. A permitted procedural exception is different from breach of a mandatory rule, and neither makes the underlying law of armed conflict irrelevant. No sources were contacted and no FOIA request or submission was made in this investigation.

The supported hook is an update-accountability problem with rules already on the books. The open reporting question is whether authorities can show that their legally significant change assessments keep pace with fielded model versions, especially across vendor and national boundaries. Calling it a proven continuous-update loophole would outrun the evidence recovered here.
