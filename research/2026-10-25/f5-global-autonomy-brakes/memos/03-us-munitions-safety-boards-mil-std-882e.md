# US munitions safety boards: a real assurance gate, with bounded public visibility

Status: source reporting completed 25 October 2026; financial award attribution belongs to the separate full-lifecycle hydration audit. This memo makes no claim that public review rules demonstrate a named weapon's compliance or effectiveness.

The strongest current document is a January 2026 engineering guide that states a technical limit rather than announcing an ethical aspiration. It develops the joint-service safety work on supervised machine learning endorsed in July 2024. Ordinary software verification is not treated as sufficient for the most safety-critical machine-learning functions. The relevant investigative question is what programs actually did with that advice, who accepted residual risk, and what evidence supported the decision. Nothing reviewed here proves that Bullfrog, AimLock or a named collaborative missile passed, failed or escaped its applicable safety board.

## The critical correction: current ML guidance recognizes an assurance deficit

The [January 2026 Machine Learning System Safety Engineering Guide](https://www.cto.mil/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/MLSSEG-8January2026-Cleared.pdf), approved by the Executive Director, Systems Engineering and Architecture, OUSW(R&E), is the current reviewed treatment. Preface viii labels tasks informational and recommended, tailorable subject to program authorities; it covers supervised learning and supersedes no law. Printed pp. 26–28 and 41 still offer no SFCI 1 ML LOR column, recommend assured interlocks/guardrails to reduce criticality, and explicitly permit SFCI 1 with elevated risk. Printed pp. 13–14 distinguish generally deterministic model execution from uncertain generalization. Printed p. 6 calls for retrained/updated models to receive the original testing level offline. This is a current assurance recommendation; actual incorporation, acceptance and version-specific compliance need program records.


The [JWSWG July 2024 endorsement and February 2024 supervised-learning white paper](https://www.cto.mil/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Memo-Endorse-LOR-Objectives-DistroA.pdf), printed pp. 8–10 and Appendix B p. 1, omit an SFCI 1 column from their LOR matrix: development assurance alone cannot adequately bound ML uncertainty without interlocks. They explicitly allow deployment with residual risk; this is no categorical prohibition. The authors recommend guardrails/interlocks and adequate assurance of those mitigations. These are supervised-learning objectives; reinforcement learning remains future work in this document. DC-22, printed p. 32, calls for repeating the data-curation LOR tasks with new training data even when source code is unchanged. The endorsement recommends formal issuance; it establishes historical endorsed guidance, not universal contractual incorporation. The 2026 guide develops this line of work; no claim of formal cancellation of the older paper is made.

This is a defensible reporting hook: **the safety establishment has published the limits of development assurance for critical machine learning, while public system-specific decisions remain difficult to inspect.** The current guide consolidates proposed processes and tasks; its news value lies in the acknowledged assurance limit and the resulting decision trail. Interlock practice predates it. It is not proof that programs ignore safety specialists.

## What SwCI does, and why 'autonomous weapons automatically equal SwCI 1' is too broad

[ASSIST's MIL-STD-882 record](https://quicksearch.dla.mil/qsDocDetails.aspx?ident_number=36027) identifies active revision E with Change 1, dated 27 September 2023; the record was updated 23 October 2026. Its scope spans system lifecycles and hardware/software. Incorporation matters: if a contract invokes the standard without specifying tasks, Sections 3 and 4 are mandatory. An acquisition's exact obligations cannot be inferred from a manufacturer's general use of the word safety.

The [official standard](https://www.cto.mil/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/MIL-STD-882E-w_CHANGE-1.pdf), §4.4 and Tables IV–VI, crosses software control category with hazard severity. Autonomous SCC 1 yields SwCI 1 for catastrophic/critical severity, but SwCI 3 for marginal and SwCI 4 for negligible severity; semi-autonomous SCC 2 can also yield SwCI 1. SwCI governs rigor, not a direct probability-of-failure score. Highest rigor includes requirements, architecture, design and code analysis plus in-depth safety testing. Incomplete SwCI 1 rigor triggers documented HIGH contribution to system risk and a PM decision between completing work and preparing formal risk acceptance. The text does not impose a universal neural-network ban or one hardware interlock architecture. Formal safety risk acceptance is not a waiver of law or permission to bypass legal review. Its 2023 change also explicitly incorporates civilian-harm mitigation into systems-engineering coordination; §4.3.8 requires lifecycle attention to software/configuration changes.

The [2012 official-hosted conference explanation](https://ndia.dtic.mil/wp-content/uploads/2012/system/track914794.pdf) describes the workflow as hazard identification, control-category assignment, severity/criticality assessment, LOR work and reassessment of risk. This is useful historical explanation; the current standard takes precedence. Readers should not collapse SCC, SwCI and the ML paper's Safety Function Criticality Index (SFCI) into one identifier.

A human authorization step is relevant only if its timing and independence provide the credited safety control for the identified hazard. A button labeled override is not evidence that an operator can perceive a problem and intervene in time. Conversely, a machine executing part of an approved action does not by itself establish absence of human judgment over force. Those are testable design and human-factors questions, not conclusions obtainable from an autonomy label.

## The board architecture is broader than AI branding

[NOSSA's official mission description](https://www.navsea.navy.mil/serve-from-netstorage/Home/NOSSA/About-NOSSA/index.html) identifies lifecycle ordnance safety authority and lists WSESRB, SSSTRP, FISTRP and the Technical Review Panel. These have distinct remits: weapon/explosives system review, software system safety, and fuze/initiation safety should not be treated as interchangeable certifications. The page establishes institutional responsibilities, not a roster of reviewed AI systems.

[Navy Medicine's WSESRB account](https://www.med.navy.mil/Navy-and-Marine-Corps-Force-Health-Protection-Command/Environmental-Health/Industrial-Hygiene/Acquisition-Technical-Support/Weapon-System-Explosive-Safety-Review-Board-WSESRB/) describes recommendations to commanders/program executives and contributions to safety-management and hazard documents. This makes a useful distinction: an independent technical review informs the authority that accepts risk; a review panel's existence does not mean risk has disappeared.

[OPNAVINST 8020.14B, 18 July 2022](https://www.secnav.navy.mil/doni/Directives/08000%20Ordnance%20Material%20Management%20and%20Support/08-00%20General%20Ordnance%20Material%20Support/8020.14B.pdf), chapter 3, states that joint-service weapon-system programs, including detect/control/engage elements, must obtain a JSWSR before low-rate initial production, deployment or fielding, and addresses shipboard testing approval. The [current Navy issuance index](https://www.secnav.navy.mil/doni/opnav.aspx?folderctid=0x012000e8af0dd9490e0547a7de7cf736393d04&rootfolder=%2Fdoni%2Fdirectives%2F08000+ordnance+material+management+and+support%2F08-00+general+ordnance+material+support) lists this instruction as active. This policy is evidence of a gate, not evidence of its execution for a named system.

[DoDI 5000.69, 10 August 2023](https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/500069p.pdf), §§1–3, requires joint reviews for systems used by two or more components, using service boards collaboratively rather than duplicating them. It expressly assigns CDAO support for AI-enabled safety reviews and AI-assurance guidance. It covers weapon-related software/firmware and unmanned platforms. It therefore contradicts a claim that AI is wholly outside the published joint weapon-safety architecture. It does not supply a public database of final decisions or establish that all relevant programs receive an identical review.

## The contract is part of the brake

The [JS-SSA Implementation Guide, Rev. B, 14 March 2018](https://www.cto.mil/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/JSSSEH-Implementation-Guide-2018.pdf), printed pp. 2–4, says acquirers must specify software-safety tasks and artifacts in the statement of work; omitted requirements jeopardize compliance. It permits tailored LOR with acquirer/developer agreement and accommodates Agile development. Its process includes lifecycle change assessment and updated safety artifacts. This is implementation guidance, not a substitute for the governing standard, component policy or actual contract.

The [Joint Software Systems Safety Engineering Handbook, version 1.0, 27 August 2010](https://www.cto.mil/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/Joint-SW-SSE-2010.pdf), contains separate COTS/non-developmental software selection, analysis, design-around and safety-case discussions. Its significance is methodological: supplier opacity and reused software are longstanding assurance problems; AI adds new difficulties rather than inventing the entire problem.

A [Naval Postgraduate School study, NPS-AM-09-145, 30 September 2009](https://dair.nps.edu/handle/123456789/2436), found vendor failures in the SSSTRP process associated with poorly structured technical-review packages and inconsistent documentation. It also examined COTS safety assessment. The authors' proposed requirements framework is historical evidence of review friction. The study predates modern commercial autonomy stacks and cannot prove current rejection rates, named-vendor failures or 2026 board practice.

The financial sweep should therefore look for deliverables such as functional hazard analyses, safety plans, hazard tracking, review-package support and software assurance—not assume every safety-engineering dollar is a legal governor. Parent awards, broad service orders, task orders and subawards must retain their distinct identities. No money is assigned to an autonomous-weapons safeguard in this memo without the separate exact-award lifecycle audit and technical attribution. Connecting a payment to a fielded safeguard requires a linked chain: exact instrument identity and complete action reconciliation; the relevant funded task/deliverable and allocable amount; version-specific hazard, mitigation and risk-acceptance evidence; and independent evidence that the same configuration reached field use. A broad safety-service award establishes neither safeguard installation nor the share spent on it.

## Determinism, uncertainty and change management

The [DoD 2021 Unmanned System Safety Engineering Precepts Guide](https://www.cto.mil/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/UxS-Precepts-2021.pdf), §§2.2–3.3 and DSP-9, describes deterministic checkpoints, runtime assurance, physical/functional partitioning and managed learning. It warns that continued learning can invalidate previous tests and that finite testing may miss outcomes. This is public engineering guidance for bounding behavior, not proof that each weapon implements these measures. A frozen neural network may execute repeatably while performing unreliably on novel inputs: execution nondeterminism, model uncertainty and inadequate environmental coverage require distinct evidence. Its examples should not be converted into a universal prescribed circuit.

The [2022 AI functional-hazard-analysis paper, endorsed 10 June 2022](https://www.cto.mil/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Memo-Endorse-FHA-DistroA.pdf) extends conventional hazard analysis to AI-supported functions and describes itself as intermediate guidance addressing a subset of AI technologies. It supplies a bridge from function and consequence to criticality. It should not be represented as a universal certification that resolves all AI cases.

[DoDM 5000.101, 9 December 2024](https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodm/5000101p.pdf) supplies AI/autonomy operational and live-fire T&E procedures, including model metrics, data governance, robustness, human-machine integration, and recording unexpected/emergent behavior. These tests investigate operational adequacy; satisfying a software process alone would not establish the same result.

The [February 2025 developmental T&E guidebook](https://www.cto.mil/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/TE_of_AIES_Guidebook_Final_26Feb25.pdf) calls for early tester involvement, continual evaluation of iterative ML refinement, testable requirements and formal methods as an augmentation. Its distinction between procedural AI and machine learning cautions against using one testing story for all AI.

[GAO-26-107009, 11 December 2025](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-107009) found gaps between DOD testing policy and leading iterative-development practices and made 13 recommendations. Its evidence concerns testing modernization across acquisition pathways. It does not establish that named safety panels waived safeguards; it does show why a published manual cannot be assumed to describe uniformly implemented practice.

[GAO-27-107837, 14 October 2026](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-27-107837) supplies a fresher implementation check. Its ten selected use cases are not a representative survey of all weapon safety boards. It reports the Responsible AI Council stopped meeting from late 2024 until August 2025, then resumed; it warns about an unchartered council lapsing again. The three filled positions out of ten authorized were for CDAO's Responsible AI Division as of February 2026, not October staffing or all safety boards. Organizational capacity limits merit scrutiny; these findings do not prove bypass of a weapon review or operational legal governor.

## Range termination demonstrates an operational brake with a narrow job

[NASA reported NAFTU's first flight on Rocket Lab's 24 January 2023 launch](https://www.nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/wallops/new-nasa-safety-system-enables-rocket-lab-launch-from-wallops/). The independent onboard termination system made autonomous flight-safety decisions. Certification was provisional for that mission; the article's expected full-certification date is not proof of later completion. This is concrete flight use of a safety function, with a public-safety remit rather than battlefield target-legality assurance.

[Vandenberg's current FTS portal](https://www.vandenberg.spaceforce.mil/Flight-Termination-System/) lists certification, mission-data validation and compliance resources, including a matrix for RCC 319-25. It emphasizes tailored program requirements and warns the portal is not a checklist. Its linked [AFTS certification roadmap](https://www.vandenberg.spaceforce.mil/Portals/18/documents/Range/FTS%20-%202026%20Update/SEAE-FTS-002%20Autonomous%20Flight%20Termination%20System%20Certification%20Roadmap.pdf?ver=unP77WfGrDFrP__OTmJdow%3D%3D) describes independent V&V and protection from single points of failure. That is a public certification architecture, not proof of any particular vendor's current approval. The earlier RCC 319-19 remains relevant to historical NASA bulletins; it should not be called the current universal requirement.

[NASA NESC bulletin 23-02, 17 March 2023](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20230001890) is a particularly useful counterexample to safety-by-label. It warns that commercial termination systems designed for public/ground safety lack some protections needed for flight-crew safety. Safety for one protected population cannot automatically be transferred to another use. [NESC bulletin 24-02, August 2024](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/tb-24-02-082324-1.pdf) similarly addresses additional software assurance and certification for crewed use. Neither establishes legal compliance of armed autonomous vehicles.

[NASA's 2017 Safeguard research](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20170009617) reports independent onboard operational-boundary monitoring, software V&V and flight testing. It is an existence example of verifiable safety-envelope research, not a fielded military legal-control claim.

## What a journalist can now establish, and what still needs reporting

The public record supports a layered account: system-safety duties; independent review institutions; contracts that should purchase specific artifacts; ML-specific assurance guidance; testing and configuration controls; and actual operational range-safety functions. It does not support either 'there are no brakes' or 'the boards ensure autonomous weapons are safe.'

Three questions now have specific records behind them:

1. For a selected weapon and software version, what criticality was assigned, what mitigations were credited, and what documentation demonstrates timely independent intervention?
2. Did the contracting instrument incorporate the joint-service supervised-ML objectives, and how were unavailable evidence or incomplete LOR recorded and accepted?
3. When training data or weights changed, which safety analyses, tests and approvals were rerun, and did that change also trigger the separate legal-review requirements?

No source was contacted; no FOIA/form was submitted. Exact system-specific review findings, risk acceptances, waivers and current deployment effectiveness were not verified. Absence from this public-source review is not evidence that a review never happened.
