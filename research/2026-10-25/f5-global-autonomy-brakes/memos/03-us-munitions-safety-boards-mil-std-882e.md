# US munitions safety boards, ML assurance and actual review friction

**Evidence cut-off:** 25 October 2026. **Status:** three editorial rounds completed; bounded findings and contextual-case disposition, not a finding that any unnamed program passed or evaded review. Author: safety-boards workstream. Public-source governance analysis only. No outreach, restricted access, operational weapon design, or external publication undertaken.

## Findings and scope

The public record supports a more interesting story than either “nobody reviews these weapons” or “a safety board has already solved autonomous targeting.” US weapon-safety institutions have formal review processes, software rigor categories, signed risk-acceptance pathways, and newly explicit guidance for machine learning. Their effectiveness for a particular weapon depends on the function and configuration reviewed, the evidence supplied, mitigation independence, scope of the approval, and continuing control of changes. Those program-specific records are often not public. Conditional test authorizations, restricted personnel participation, battery-use approvals and funded safety-document work are meaningful observed governance even without a complete model-to-fielded-weapon chain. The claim must stay at the level each source supports.

Three central corrections are necessary:

1. **A weapon called autonomous is not automatically Software Criticality Index 1 (SwCI 1).** MIL-STD-882E assesses a software function’s contribution to a hazard using both severity and control characteristics. The January 2026 ML guide expressly warns against deriving the category from an entire system’s autonomous/semi-autonomous label. [S01, S02]
2. **Learned does not mean non-deterministic at inference.** The 2026 guide explains that training can be stochastic and output probabilistic while execution is deterministic. Repeatability for identical inputs does not establish predictable or safe behavior over unfamiliar inputs, distribution shifts, adversarial conditions, or operator interactions. [S02]
3. **Safety review is neither an ethical governor nor wholly separate from civilian protection.** It addresses hazardous functions, including unauthorized or inadvertent launch and targeting-related system behavior. MIL-STD-882E Change 1 explicitly incorporates civilian-harm considerations while distinguishing other disciplines’ responsibilities. Engineering safety evidence does not itself decide the legality of a particular attack. [S01, S03, S18]

An important recent development is the official **January 2026 Machine Learning System Safety Engineering Guide**. The absence of a highest-criticality ML assurance column, recommendation for independently assured constraints, and residual-risk caveat were **already present in the 2024 LOR paper**. The 2026 contribution is a broader implementation guide with an eleven-part safety-process structure, an explicit partial-LOR-credit discussion and a procurement appendix addressing unavailable proprietary evidence. This is expanded guidance, not a newly binding policy. Its preface calls the tasks informational and tailorable; publication does not establish universal mandatory certification or program compliance. [S02, S05]

## 1. What MIL-STD-882E actually requires

The active baseline is **MIL-STD-882E with Change 1, 27 September 2023**, not simply the original 2012 edition. ASSIST lists it active; the authoritative PDF cover, rather than stale internal PDF metadata, establishes its revision. When cited by a contract or solicitation, sections 3 and 4 are mandatory; additional numbered tasks apply when invoked. The standard is a contract-linked safety framework, not a stand-alone criminal prohibition on unreviewed AI. [S01]

Its software matrix is function-specific:

| Software control category | Catastrophic hazard | Critical | Marginal | Negligible |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| SCC 1 | SwCI 1 | 1 | 3 | 4 |
| SCC 2 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
| SCC 3 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 4 |
| SCC 4 | 3 | 4 | 4 | 4 |
| SCC 5 | 5 | 5 | 5 | 5 |

Source: Table V, printed p.17. SCC describes control and independent intervention characteristics; it is not a scale of how futuristic a system is. **Even SCC 2 remains SwCI 1 for a catastrophic hazard.** Therefore neither “there is a human in the loop” nor “we added an interlock” alone establishes the resulting SwCI. The full definitions and hazard context must be applied. The software criticality matrix is expressly **not itself a risk assessment**. [S01]

At the highest LOR, Table V calls for requirements, architecture, design and code analysis plus in-depth safety-specific testing. Lower categories reduce that prescribed analysis scope. Table VI links missing or unspecified LOR work to software’s contribution to risk: HIGH for SwCI 1, SERIOUS for 2, MEDIUM for 3 and LOW for 4. Management can resource the missing work or pursue documented risk acceptance; it is not accurate to turn an incomplete LOR into an automatic statutory ban. Conversely, this is not an unrestricted PM waiver: applicable acquisition authorities and, for high/serious risks, user concurrence govern acceptance before exposure. Changes that introduce new or higher risks reopen the process. [S01]

**Reporting consequence:** request the actual Functional Hazard Analysis, SCC/SwCI assignments, approved LOR table, completion evidence, hazard tracking records, signed acceptance and configuration/version identifiers. A vendor’s declaration of “MIL-STD-882 compliance” is not an adequate substitute.

## 2. ML guidance: a deliberate accommodation, not proof that neural networks cannot be deployed

The Joint Weapon Safety Working Group endorsed AI Functional Hazard Analysis guidance in June 2022, then supervised-ML LOR objectives in July 2024. The 2026 guide consolidates and expands that trajectory. Its terminology uses **Function Control Category (FCC)** and **Safety Function Criticality Index (SFCI)** for ML; these should not silently be treated as a change to the text of MIL-STD-882E’s SCC/SwCI matrix. The guide’s Table 2-1 retains the same severity-by-control entries. [S02, S04, S05]

The 2024 endorsement and 2026 guide do not supply an SFCI 1 assurance column. They explain that the contemplated assurance tasks alone are not commensurate with the risk of unconstrained highest-criticality ML functionality. They recommend verified independent constraints and a less demanding resulting category where the hazard analysis supports it. **They do not categorically prohibit SFCI 1 deployment**; inadequately assured functionality must instead be addressed through risk characterization and acceptance. A proposed constraint must itself receive appropriate assurance. [S02, S05]

The 2022 FHA paper defines a safety control entity functionally. Depending on policy and circumstances it can involve an operator, hardware, firmware, software or another AI function. Thus “an independent safeguard must always be a physical hardware kill switch” is too broad. Independence, timely intervention, common-cause considerations and assurance matter; merely naming a second AI model a watchdog does not establish those properties. [S04]

The new guide adds data curation, algorithm analysis and human interfaces to traditional requirements/design/code/T&E work. It recommends independent reviewer and test roles for higher-criticality functions, while addressing retraining, data shifts and model configuration through lifecycle hazard analysis. These are meaningful governance expectations, not an assertion that source inspection makes every learned decision explainable. [S02]

### Tailoring and proprietary evidence

The 2018 Joint Services Software Safety Authorities implementation guide is **Rev B, 14 March 2018**, separate from the older Joint Software Systems Safety Engineering Handbook. It supplies preferred LOR tasks, calls for acquirer/developer agreement and SSWG approval of tailoring, recommends relevant review-authority concurrence, and says schedule/budget-driven omissions may increase risk. Its example permits a justified narrower requirements-review task for rapid acquisition; it does not authorize an undocumented deletion of all software review. [S06]

The 2026 ML guide explicitly anticipates commercial developers not transferring source code, design specifications or training datasets. Its procurement appendix suggests obtaining contracted evidence, data/provenance documentation and independent test datasets when the underlying training data are unavailable. Example contract clauses contain fields to be specified, not a universal government entitlement to every model weight or dataset. Appendix B, printed pp.116–118, specifically offers data cards, provenance/curation documentation, contracted exploratory-analysis results and government-created test sets where proprietary training data are unavailable. Model weights appear among examples of deliverables the contracting parties must specify; the guide does not make their transfer universally mandatory. Hidden training data leave uncertainty; independent testing is not proof that this uncertainty disappears. [S02]

The guide’s partial-LOR-credit discussion requires technical-authority discussion, risk analysis, justified assessment and hazard documentation. It calls this a **temporary measure**, not a replacement for comprehensive assessment. Under the guide’s recommended process, changes to models and operating context prompt renewed analysis/testing and updated artifacts; it is therefore wrong to assume a past approval automatically covers retraining or new autonomy functions. [S02]

The 2014 joint Safety Data Package preparation guide calls for a specific software version, safety-function description, hazard tracking, planned/completed analysis and test evidence, and signed risk-acceptance records. It permits tailoring with the service coordinator. It does not require the entire technical drawing package as the standard submission. Its historical references/contacts should not be used as current organizational facts without verification. [S09]

A current Navy Newport capability page explicitly lists **inadvertent and unauthorized launch analysis**, software/firmware safety, own-ship/target-ship safety and warhead safety. This usefully defeats the claim that the field addresses only warehouse explosions; it still does not show a machine deciding distinction or proportionality. [S18]

The Army’s October 2024 DevSecSafOps account describes checking hazards, mitigations, SCC/SwCI/LOR, tests and problem reports as software releases evolve. This is evidence that practitioners recognize continuous delivery, not only a one-time acquisition milestone. It does not demonstrate how consistently any particular contractor executes those checks. [S19]

## 4. Attributable cases: authorization, conditions and limitations

### C01 — Mjölnir/SkyRaider: disclosed temporary live-test authorization, July 2025

A Marine Corps release dated 9 July reports a 3 July 2025 Camp Lejeune exercise. **NAVAIR issued an Interim Flight Clearance (IFC)** after reviewing drone configuration, range control and mitigations, permitting SkyRaider to carry/release a live munition. The release expressly says developmental Mjölnir had not completed full WSESRB certification and that the exercise was a test/demo, not fleet fielding. This is evidence of a conditional experimental pathway and potentially functioning governance—not, by itself, a safety bypass. [S20]

The release calls the detonation premature during free fall and contrasts it with intended point detonation. Although it mentions an airburst capability elsewhere, it does **not** call this event a planned airburst. It supplies no cause, injury determination or formal investigation/disposition. No AI target selection is documented. A separate Neros Archer event describes operator-commanded detonation; conflating it with the Mjölnir event would be wrong. The actual IFC, conditions and hazard acceptance are not public in this source. [S20]

The Navy’s experimentation guide independently explains that IFC is normally an interim airworthiness authorization, with configuration/usage limits and multidisciplinary review. This corroborates the kind of process, **not the contents of this particular clearance**. [S21]

### C02 — Trident Elding 2022: personnel safety releases declined, according to Raytheon

A public 2023 Aviation Week award submission by Raytheon program director Daniel T. Dyring states that USAF and NNMSB declined releases for **USAF personnel to operate** the NASAMS demonstration system. The alternative was training and simulated engagements during tracking/practice events. An independent official AFRL account confirms the summer 2022 experiment at Andøya proceeded and reports successful live target engagements, but does not confirm the refusal or its reasons. [S33] This is a concrete, attributable account of a safety gate affecting participation—not proof that NASAMS was rejected, that the entire demonstration was cancelled, or that AI caused the refusal. The submission attributes the refusal to a finding that no safety-release precedents existed because NASAMS was not a USG Program of Record. That is **Raytheon’s stated rationale**; the underlying government decision and reasoning were not obtained. The submission records AFRL public-affairs clearance for its release; that clearance is not independent validation of every company claim. [S22, PDF pp.10–11]

### C03 — Iver4 submarine AUV batteries: approval of a particular hazard domain

L3Harris’s 12 November 2024 account says WSESRB approved the lithium-ion batteries used with Iver4 900 AUVs for submarine use. This is a contractor-attributed example of safety certification enabling autonomous-platform adoption. It is **battery/submarine-use approval**, not approval of autonomous lethal target selection. The underlying Navy board letter and configuration limits were not found. Do not repeat the vendor’s “first/only” market claims as independently established. [S23]

### C04 — Software safety submissions: documented historical process weakness, not a current failure rate

A 2009 Naval Postgraduate School report and Joey Rivera’s December 2010 dissertation document historical review-quality problems. The dissertation analyzed **86 SSSTRP failure reports from FY2007–09**, containing 177 issues, and defined failure as a review temporarily or permanently halting software acquisition. Crucially, clean reviews did not generate these reports: this is a failure-only sample, not the denominator of all reviews, and 86 reports do not establish 86 distinct systems. Only declassified results were available; without the complete classified set, the sample’s representativeness could not be established. It found inconsistent reporting and concerns about reviewer turnover and expertise. These are historical academic findings, not a current Navy performance audit. Neither this sample nor Afterburner marketing establishes a contemporary 75% failure rate. Named decisions and a current denominator remain missing. [S13, S34, printed pp17–20]

### C05 — Navy N231-030: AI helping prepare safety evidence, not an AI trigger interlock

The official Navy SBIR topic sought model-based engineering tools to organize and connect advanced-munition safety artifacts across FISTRP, SSSTRP and WSESRB. Official award pages identify INSPIRD and Penta Research Phase I projects. These are concrete safety-workflow investments, including AI-assisted extraction/search of technical information. Neither the topic nor the award pages demonstrate a fielded runtime restraint on weapon firing. A 2024 Phase II award to Penta is also confirmed by an official SBIR record, and the actual Penta page of the Navy STP guide describes AIM-SAFE as being developed, with planned prototype deliveries and intended missile-program transition targets. Its milestone chart lists planned prototype deliveries, not demonstrated completion; its contract-end line and later milestone dates are not a reliable current contract-status record. A transition target is not proof of deployment or acceptance. Full contract lifecycle funding remains for the coordinator to verify. [S24–S26, S35–S36]

## 6. What the evidence cannot yet answer

**Highest priority evidence gaps:**

- Actual board findings, pending actions, conditions, waivers and signed risk acceptances for Bullfrog, AIMLOCK or the cited Eglin autonomous-weapon efforts. No public docket was located here. Non-discovery does not prove absence, exemption, approval, or rejection.
- Whether a particular learned targeting function was treated as SCC/FCC 1, what severity was assigned, whether the developer and government agreed, and how independence was demonstrated.
- Who could inspect proprietary code, training data and weights; what rights were contracted; what independent evidence substituted; whether model updates changed the approved baseline.
- Mjölnir IFC and test-risk records; subsequent full WSESRB disposition; formal determination of the premature event.
- Trident Elding safety-release refusal letter, independent confirmation of Raytheon’s stated lack-of-precedent rationale, and the authorizations governing other participants.
- Whether the 2026 informational ML guide has been incorporated into specific solicitations, task orders or service mandatory instructions; which programs used partial LOR credit or accepted SFCI 1 risk.
- Current, independently verifiable review pass/fail rates; staffing, review duration and backlogs; denominator by test/fielding stage; contractor conflict-management and review independence.

**Most productive next reporting question:** not “Why does the military allow non-deterministic weapons despite mandatory proof of safety?” but **“For this specific hazardous AI function, what evidence did the responsible authority require, what did it not obtain, what restrictions survived into field use, and who signed acceptance of the residual risk?”**


### Editorial disposition: contextual sidebar, not a top-three autonomous-AI failure exposé

**The Mjölnir case demonstrates a named conditional authorization in actual developmental use, and explains the difference between permission to test and permission to field. It is not presently evidence of an autonomous-AI safety failure or an AI-specific approval gap.** NAVAIR’s temporary clearance enabled the 2025 experiment, and the Marine Corps reported an early detonation. The strongest benign explanation is that a properly bounded developmental test identified a problem; the available evidence does not establish otherwise. [S20]

Do not juxtapose that event with ML risk-acceptance guidance in a way that implies an unproved causal or approval connection. An allegation about a particular AI function’s unsafe behavior or accepted residual risk needs evidence identifying that function and decision. The absence of that evidence limits the AI-specific allegation; it does not erase the documented test conditions or require every governance finding to prove a complete fielding chain. The NASAMS personnel-release refusal is also historical and contractor-attributed, not an AI failure case. The Penta award is direct, recent AI safety-workflow research investment, but its planned prototype is not a delivered runtime restraint. [S22, S35–S36]

The defensible direct AI finding is the **content and limits of the 2024–26 assurance framework**: acknowledged highest-criticality assurance limits, informational/tailorable guidance, evidence-access uncertainty, and documented routes for characterizing and accepting residual risk. Whether any specific fielded weapon actually relied on those routes remains an open reporting question, not this memo’s discovery. This workstream supports standards/accountability analysis, named examples of conditions and friction, and specific reporting questions. The evidence does not support an AI-bypass scoop.

## 8. Additional operational and implementation evidence

### A flown autonomous safety function and its protected population

[NASA reported NAFTU’s first flight on Rocket Lab’s 24 January 2023 launch](https://www.nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/wallops/new-nasa-safety-system-enables-rocket-lab-launch-from-wallops/). Its independent onboard system made autonomous flight-termination decisions to protect the public and ground population. Certification was provisional for that mission; the article’s expected later full-certification date does not prove completion. This is observed flight use of a safety function, not an inference from a patent or board’s existence. It does not assure the legality of battlefield target selection.

[NASA NESC bulletin 23-02, 17 March 2023](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20230001890) warns that commercial termination systems designed for public/ground safety lack some protections needed for flight-crew safety. [NESC bulletin 24-02, August 2024](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/tb-24-02-082324-1.pdf) addresses additional software assurance for crewed reuse. This demonstrates why the protected population and operating context matter: a valid safety approval cannot automatically be transferred to a different hazard or use. No design details are reproduced.

The [Vandenberg FTS portal](https://www.vandenberg.spaceforce.mil/Flight-Termination-System/) lists certification, mission-data validation and program-tailoring resources, including an RCC 319-25 compliance matrix. The [linked roadmap](https://www.vandenberg.spaceforce.mil/Portals/18/documents/Range/FTS%20-%202026%20Update/SEAE-FTS-002%20Autonomous%20Flight%20Termination%20System%20Certification%20Roadmap.pdf) has substantial indexed text on independent V&V but returned 403 for full readback. The folder name does not independently establish its publication date. RCC 319-19 is relevant to historical NASA material, not a verified current universal requirement.

[NASA’s 2017 Safeguard research](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20170009617) reports independent onboard operational-boundary monitoring, software verification and flight tests. This is an existence example of a tested safety-envelope approach, not a military weapons legal-control claim.

### Testing and organizational implementation

[DoDM 5000.101, 9 December 2024](https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodm/5000101p.pdf), has substantial official indexed text covering AI/autonomy operational and live-fire test procedures, including model metrics, data governance, robustness, human-machine integration and recording unexpected behavior. Full-file retrieval returned 403 in this lane. These accessible provisions establish a distinct testing framework, not a complete review of every current clause or proof that a system passed.

The [February 2025 developmental T&E guidebook](https://www.cto.mil/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/TE_of_AIES_Guidebook_Final_26Feb25.pdf), read in full, recommends early tester involvement, continual evaluation during iterative ML refinement, testable requirements and formal methods as an augmentation. Its distinction between procedural AI and machine learning cautions against one testing story for every AI system.

[GAO-26-107009, 11 December 2025](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-107009) found gaps between DOD testing policies and leading iterative-development practices and made 13 recommendations. It examines testing modernization, not named-board waivers. [GAO-27-107837, 14 October 2026](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-27-107837) provides a fresher organizational implementation check: the Responsible AI Council stopped meeting from late 2024 until August 2025, then resumed. Three filled posts of ten authorized concerned CDAO’s Responsible AI Division as of February 2026, not all safety boards or October staffing. Its ten selected use cases are not a representative sample of all weapons review. These findings support scrutiny of implementation capacity without proving an individual review was bypassed.

### Existing software assurance and lifecycle controls

The [2010 Joint Software Systems Safety Engineering Handbook](https://www.cto.mil/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/Joint-SW-SSE-2010.pdf) separately addresses COTS/non-developmental software selection, assessment, design-around and safety cases. Supplier opacity and software reuse are longstanding assurance problems. The [August 2021 UxS Precepts Guide](https://www.cto.mil/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/UxS-Precepts-2021.pdf) discusses deterministic checkpoints, runtime assurance, physical/functional partitioning and managed learning; it warns that continued learning can invalidate prior tests. Its older policy references require checking against later editions, and its examples are guidance rather than universal prescribed architecture.

Formal HIGH system-safety risk acceptance under applicable authority is not a waiver of law or permission to bypass legal weapons review. Likewise, repeatable model execution does not establish reliable generalization, adequate human intervention or lawful operational use. The safety record and legal-review record can inform each other while addressing distinct questions.

### Financial attribution proportional to the claim

An exact instrument identity and complete, reconciled action history are needed for a lifecycle obligation figure. A claim that an allocable amount bought a particular safeguard additionally needs the funded task/deliverable and scope linkage. A claim that the safeguard was installed and effective in field use needs appropriate configuration, test and deployment evidence. These are progressively stronger claims: a documented safety-workflow award or real test condition remains reportable even when the later field-effectiveness chain is incomplete. A broad safety-service order does not establish that every dollar purchased an installed autonomous-weapons restraint.
