# Five Eyes and AUKUS: assurance is a lifecycle obligation, with an implementation gap

F5 original reporting; public-source cutoff 25 October 2026. This dossier covers the UK, Australia and Canada; US controls are covered separately. It does not imply exhaustive coverage of New Zealand or every national programme. Sources were checked with free web retrieval; no paid Exa was used in this workstream.

The strongest defensible finding is a tension between continuous improvement and specific assurance. Shield AI has given Parliament a named example of V-BAT approval friction and software-update reapprovals; the aviation regulator separately acknowledges adaptive-AI assurance difficulties. MOD's CARRION procurement notice adds a buyer-side case: rebuilding incumbent safety/assurance evidence is cited as a reason for a proposed direct award. These are different evidence tiers, none establishing refusal to disclose weights, unlawful bypass or absent operational legal control.

## UK: a mandatory lifecycle duty, followed by adopted assurance guidance

In [written evidence DEF0035, dated 27 July 2026](https://committees.parliament.uk/writtenevidence/168308/html/), Shield AI tells Parliament that unsynchronised MAA/CAA responsibilities and duplicate assurance create cost/delay (§77). It says iterative changes informed by V-BAT operations in Ukraine produce disproportionate reapproval burdens (§§78–79). For UK V-BAT operations, it describes ambiguous approval authority and an **informal workaround to secure a Delegated Duty Holder to accept operational risk** (§§81–82). This is a concrete named-company account, stronger than a generic bottleneck claim, but still **interested-party testimony**, not an independently verified regulator finding. It supplies no approval dossier or regulator response and does not attribute the difficulty to withheld training data/weights. Nor does delegated risk acceptance, without the governing decision record, establish evasion of required safety or legal review. Its proposed distinction between minor and major updates (§86) makes change-control thresholds a useful reporting lead.

The MAA's [RN 2025/04, dated 10 October 2025](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/68f0b97c1c9076042263ef2a/MAA_RN_2025_04.pdf), separately sets out an **informative, immediately effective statement of its position**, rather than a new binding regulatory rule. For AI affecting airworthiness, it calls for evidence proportionate to failure risk and safety-case treatment of uncertainty. It recommends initially fixed models within a defined operating domain and notes conventional safety monitors can bound behaviour (§7). For non-certified UAS, adaptive safety-related AI may present an assurance deficit requiring technical measures such as manual overrides or operational mitigations (§11). It also says testing every foreseeable condition is infeasible and cannot replace development assurance (§13). This is functional guidance, not a disclosed V-BAT assessment or proof that a particular safeguard worked. Read alongside Shield AI's testimony, it frames the concrete question: how did the authority, duty holder and vendor agree the acceptable configuration, evidence and update boundaries?

An independent regulator record supplies a necessary counterweight. ENAC's [authorised-operator register](https://moduliweb.enac.gov.it/applicazioni/sapr/APR_Lista_Unificata.asp?Ricerca=2026), row **53278**, lists **Global Sat LTD / Shield AI V-BAT**, authorisation **2026/IT-1139/AO/004/Rev.00**, dated **8 June 2026**, BVLOS **yes**, expiry **31 July 2026**. This is verified, bounded flight-authorisation evidence, not universal acceptance of V-BAT or autonomous weapon engagement approval. It prevents the UK testimony being inflated into a claim that the platform cannot obtain European aviation permission. The limited authorisation had expired by this investigation's cutoff; this row does not establish renewal.

## CARRION: an assurance dependency in the buyer's own procurement case

MOD's [13 October 2026 UK5 notice, DDAITF/011](https://www.sell2wales.gov.wales/search/show/search_view.aspx?ID=OCT666902&catID=), **OCID ocds-h6vhtk-078564** (original Find a Tender publication: 12 October; Sell2Wales mirror: 13 October), proposes **£4,977,790 excluding VAT** for Applied Intuition mission software/integration. The contract is **pending**, with estimated signature **27 October 2026**, future at the cutoff. MOD argues that switching from the Project LOW baseline would require interfaces, testing, training and safety/assurance/configuration evidence to be rebuilt, adding cost, delay and risk. This is direct buyer evidence of an assurance-related switching dependency; it does not prove anti-competitive illegality, failed certification or an executed award. MOD also says the limited prototype phase should inform future competition and avoid lock-in. The notice excludes air vehicles, effectors and airworthiness/certification activities; February 2027 exercise work remains prospective.

The related [8 October 2026 DDAITF/014 systems-integrator notice](https://www.sell2wales.gov.wales/search/show/search_view.aspx?ID=OCT665439&catID=), **OCID ocds-h6vhtk-078239**, names **Rowden Technologies Ltd**, proposed **£3,610,000 excluding VAT**, and likewise cites incumbent integration knowledge and assurance/schedule risks if transferred. It records a **pending** contract despite an estimated 20 October signature: a passed estimate does not verify signing. Its proposed MOD-sovereign open architecture and future competition are intentions, not demonstrated interchangeability. These separate notice values are not combined into outlays or used to infer the cost of operational brakes.

[JSP 936 Part 1](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6735fc89f6920bfb5abc7b62/JSP936_Part1.pdf), **Dependable Artificial Intelligence (AI) in Defence**, is V1.1, November 2024. Its preface applies to all Defence projects containing AI, across domains; mandatory directions and recommended practice must be distinguished. Its cover note expressly declines to publish a list of acceptable/unacceptable capabilities. Compliance depends on development and use, with context-appropriate human involvement and retained responsibility. Therefore it is inaccurate to treat JSP 936 as a single product test that commercial autonomy either universally passes or fails. The [publication page](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/jsp-936-dependable-artificial-intelligence-ai-in-defence-part-1-directive) dates release to 13 November 2024 and describes governance, development and assurance across the lifecycle.

The September 2025 [Turing/Accenture study](https://www.turing.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2025-09/defence_ai_assurance.pdf), coordinated with MOD's Defence AI and Autonomy Unit and Defence AI Centre, identifies implementation bottlenecks: overlapping rules, uncertain responsibility, scarce qualified people, inconsistent mission metrics and inadequate infrastructure. Company secrecy and government developers' reluctance to explain models can obstruct explainability (printed p.16). Its supplier transparency clauses, inherited assurance and bounded iteration are recommendations; subsequent DAIC adoption is documented below. Printed pp.33–34 pair fine-tuning with authorised boundaries and commanders' risk. The report's lab-ready drone illustration is explicitly **fictitious**, not a documented blocked programme. Neither it nor JSP establishes the hypothesised named-vendor failure. Full-PDF checks did not find the six named US vendors identified as failing JSP 936. No blanket requirement to surrender weights was established. The useful question is what evidence MOD demanded, received, rejected or waived.

An earlier [CETaS third-party assurance report](https://cetas.turing.ac.uk/publications/assurance-third-party-ai-systems-uk-national-security), 17 January 2024, supports the underlying procurement problem: outsourcing can reduce direct oversight; supply-chain opacity, security testing and ethical due diligence require structured evidence. Its system-card framework was proposed for trial across national security. Hypothetical case studies are not named contract disputes or rejection decisions. This is primary research about assurance practice, rather than a MOD certification register.

The [Defence AI Playbook](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/defence-artificial-intelligence-ai-playbook), 1 February 2024, offers examples of AI already used or under development. It helps identify real use cases but cannot certify an autonomous weapon's safety or legal acceptability. The [10 June 2026 senior-leadership letter](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/putting-artificial-intelligence-ai-at-the-heart-of-uk-defence/putting-artificial-intelligence-ai-at-the-heart-of-uk-defence) calls for faster AI adoption and workforce skills. Reading these together supports a documented institutional pressure for speed; it does not prove a safety waiver.

**A significant subsequent implementation step:** the [DAIC AI Assurance Framework](https://digital.mod.uk/policy-rules-standards-and-guidance/aiph/the-defence-ai-centre-ai-assurance-framework), published 27 February 2026, now supplies people/process/evidence/technology guidance to operationalise JSP 936. The companion [responsibility page](https://www.digital.mod.uk/policy-rules-standards-and-guidance/aiph/responsibility-for-assuring-ai-in-defence) states that suppliers must have assurance processes and MOD owns JSP compliance; RAISOs provide organisational oversight and SROs own programme risks. This is stronger implementation evidence than the 2025 recommendations alone, though it remains guidance about process rather than an audited compliance rate.

The current [system-card documentation page](https://www.digital.mod.uk/policy-rules-standards-and-guidance/aiph/development/guidance-on-system-cards/documenting-storing-and-sharing-system-cards) says DAIC broadly adopted the Turing template, with modifications; the **template is optional**. Cards should evolve with the system, keep earlier versions and record technical context/legal authority. It also states that **MOD currently has no central repository for system cards**, with teams storing them internally. This is a concrete evidence-management limitation, not proof of lost records, missing cards or failed controls. The [system-card overview](https://www.digital.mod.uk/policy-rules-standards-and-guidance/aiph/development/guidance-on-system-cards) and [model-card guidance](https://www.digital.mod.uk/policy-rules-standards-and-guidance/aiph/development/guidance-on-model-cards), both dated 27 February 2026, distinguish a model from its deployed system and warn that model cards alone do not supply all needed information.

The [deployment sign-off guidance](https://www.digital.mod.uk/policy-rules-standards-and-guidance/aiph/assurance/step-by-step-guide-to-assuring-ai/signing-off-and-deploying-an-ai-system) requires MOD confidence in tested/mitigated risks and the SRO's satisfaction before deployment. The [live-system monitoring page](https://www.digital.mod.uk/policy-rules-standards-and-guidance/aiph/assurance/step-by-step-guide-to-assuring-ai/monitoring-a-live-ai-system) identifies data/context/model drift and new operators as risk sources, calls for risk-register updates and SRO escalation when pausing/changing is needed, and says significant changes may require reapproval. These are explicit lifecycle brakes; whether they worked for an individual updated weapon remains a case-level question. A change-management assurance reapproval must also be distinguished from a legal weapons review.

The [20 February 2026 regulatory review call](https://www.gov.uk/government/calls-for-evidence/review-of-mod-legislation-governing-autonomous-systems/review-of-the-regulatory-system-governing-uncrewed-and-autonomous-systems-in-defence), closed 13 March, directly sought examples of excessive time/cost burdens and barriers to testing/trialling/deployment. It acknowledges complexity from technological change and civilian/military regulatory overlap; it expressly excludes commercial/procurement regulations. The consultation supports reporting on recognised friction, but is not a published set of accepted allegations or proof of specific regulatory changes.

The DAIC-commissioned May 2026 [Resilient Defence AI](https://www.turing.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2026-05/the_alan_turing_institute_daic_-_resilient_defence_ai_report.pdf) adds a practical dimension: power, cooling, communications and supply chains affect operational reliability, and interfaces can obscure degradation from operators (printed pp.35–36). Annex B proposes sustainability additions to system cards. This is original research and proposed practice, not a revised mandatory standard or named certification failure. Its relevance is that a brake reliant on effective human judgment also depends on whether the human can perceive changed system conditions. The PDF was too large for the web parser but retrieved successfully from the public publisher via ordinary HTTP and read locally; it is cited, not rehosted.

## A future November start is not an October deployment

The [Dstl/UK Defence Innovation Novel Autonomy and Robotics Phase 1 notice](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/novel-autonomy-and-robotics-phase-1) was published 14 July 2026 and updated 20 August. Its first-stage deadline was 11 August and invited second-stage deadline 17 September; feedback is listed for **31 October 2026**, with contracts aimed to start **November 2026**. Both are future at this dossier's cutoff. The £600,000 Phase 1 pot and £60,000 maximum proposal value are funding availability, not verified obligations or outlays. Starting TRL 1–2 and a maximum three-month project duration make this early science, not evidence of fielded weapon certification. The [competition document](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/novel-autonomy-and-robotics-phase-1/novel-autonomy-robotics-competition-document) describes an incubator and distinguishes phases; its terms cannot be silently converted into award results.

## Australia: separate human factors, ethical assurance and vessel certification

DSTG's [Operationalisation of Agent Transparency](https://www.dst.defence.gov.au/publication/operationalisation-agent-transparency-and-evidence-its-impact-key-human-autonomy-teaming), **DST-Group-TR-3413, October 2017**, is much older than the proposed 2023–26 investigation window. It reviews human-autonomy teaming evidence: more transparency can aid performance but excess information can impair it, and the optimal form/amount is unresolved. It supplies a reason to test actual human control rather than equate an interface with effective oversight. It is neither a current weapon approval nor a finding that every proprietary system is unassurable.

[A Method for Ethical AI in Defence, DSTG-TR-3786](https://www.dst.defence.gov.au/sites/default/files/publications/documents/A%20Method%20for%20Ethical%20AI%20in%20Defence%20Brochure.pdf) offers a checklist, risk matrix and Legal and Ethical Assurance Program Plan (LEAPP). The latter would expose contractor planning and support internal decisions including Article 36 reviews. Crucially, the official brochure says the report describes workshop outcomes and **does not represent an official Defence position**. It demonstrates developed assurance methods, not automatic implementation on every Australian weapon. The full report endpoint returned 403 in this run; the public official brochure was readable and is the bounded basis for this description.

The TAS [Australian Code of Practice for autonomous/remotely operated vessels](https://rasgateway.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Australian-Code-of-Practice-for-Autonomous-and-Remotely-Operated-Vessels-1.pdf) is **Edition 1, April 2022**, despite the 2026 hosting path. It was developed principally for domestic commercial vessels and certification/entry into service in Australian waters. It distinguishes vessel-risk schemes and preserves other applicable obligations. Participation by naval or defence organisations in consultation does not turn it into approval of weapon selection/engagement. Its mixed maritime ecosystem is valuable precisely because civilian navigational safety, naval seaworthiness and weapon legality need different evidence.

The Defence [SDIP 7: Test and evaluation, certification and systems assurance](https://www.defence.gov.au/sites/default/files/2024-02/SDIP7-Test-evaluation-certification-systems-assurance.pdf) identifies objective test evidence and industrial/workforce capacity as essential, including for autonomous systems. This is a named capability-development priority, not a disclosed list of accepted/rejected AI products. The [Defence Trailblazer RAS/AI public project list](https://dtb.solutions/category/news/rd-project/ras-ai/) identifies an Ocius/UNSW operator-control project dated 3 July 2026 and other university/industry collaborations. Project announcements establish activity, not verified certification outcomes. A publicly indexed Trailblazer roadmap carried inconsistent commercial-in-confidence markings; it was excluded from the evidence base rather than redistributed.

## AUKUS: interoperable trials are an evidence category of their own

Australia's [capability-achievements page](https://www.defence.gov.au/defence-activities/programs-initiatives/aukus-advanced-capabilities/capability-achievements) describes reciprocal remote control at Autonomous Warrior 2024, acoustic communications and commander-directed AI/autonomy activity at Talisman Sabre 2025, and later exercises. The official [Maritime Big Play factsheet](https://www.defence.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-06/Maritime%20Big%20Play.pdf) explains common control and underwater communications. These are tangible integration experiments; they do not disclose the hazard assessment, legal-review version, or stop/override performance in failed communications. They cannot establish either a complete absence or an effective deployment of weapon brakes.

The [30 May 2026 ministerial statement](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/aukus-defence-ministerial-joint-statement-30-may-2026/aukus-defence-ministerial-joint-statement-30-may-2026) announces a first Pillar II Signature Project for UUV payloads/enabling systems, **delivery starting in 2027**. That delivery date matters. Its discussion of a licence-free trade environment concerns export/supply collaboration, not waiver of weapon safety or legal review. A [UK parliamentary answer, 20 April 2026](https://questions-statements.parliament.uk/written-questions/detail/2026-04-10/125418), describes HyFliTE as shared testing of hypersonic systems through 2028, not an AI-specific certification regime. Hypersonic test acceleration should not be used as a proxy for neural-network assurance.

## Canada: calibrated trust and sovereignty, with no disclosed vendor rejection

The [DND/CAF AI Strategy, 2024](https://publications.gc.ca/collections/collection_2024/mdn-dnd/D2-633-2024-eng.pdf) combines an AI-enabled-by-2030 ambition with a dedicated ethics, safety and trust line of effort. It requires lifecycle treatment of ethical/security risks and recognises scarce AI skills. It is evidence of commitments and recognised constraints; the strategy alone does not show programme-by-programme compliance.

The current [IDEaS Cognition and Trust challenge](https://www.canada.ca/en/department-national-defence/programs/defence-ideas/element/innovation-networks/challenge/cognition-and-trust-real-time-dynamic-calibration-for-human-autonomy-teams.html) seeks independent, real-time trust assessment/calibration and explicitly links independence to technological sovereignty and reduced dependencies. It offers up to C$1 million per proposal through 31 March 2028 as non-repayable contribution funding. That is a solicitation ceiling, not paid-out funding. The demand for independence concerns the trust-management framework; it is not proof of access to a particular vendor's weights or a decision to bar US autonomy. Its required testing is a meaningful process requirement, with eventual effectiveness still unverified.

The [2026 counter-UAS sandbox](https://www.canada.ca/en/department-national-defence/programs/defence-ideas/element/sandboxes/challenge/counter-uncrewed-aerial-systems-sandbox-2026.html) lists participating technologies. A [separate 2025 urban sandbox](https://www.canada.ca/en/department-national-defence/programs/defence-ideas/element/sandboxes/challenge/counter-uncrewed-aerial-systems-sandbox-2025.html) is detect-only. Neither a participant roster nor a controlled trial establishes operational rules of engagement. The DRDC-hosted [Human Control of Autonomous Systems](https://pubs.drdc-rddc.gc.ca/BASIS/pcandid/www/frepub/DDW?W%3DSYSNUM=815744) catalogue record is **DRDC-RDDC-2022-C382**, but gives publication date **1 March 2021**. Treat the identifier/date mismatch explicitly; it is contract research about control concepts, not a 2026 official legal determination.

## Evidence assessment and reporting next steps

| Proposition | Assessment | What would materially advance it |
|---|---|---|
| Allied governance demands more than aggregate model accuracy | Supported by directives and original research | Completed mission-specific cards and test reports |
| UK assurance has documented implementation bottlenecks | Supported by research and Shield AI's named parliamentary testimony; not an audited rejection rate | Regulator response, decision record and revised evidence for the V-BAT case |
| Named US commercial stacks cannot pass JSP 936 without training data/weights | Unverified hypothesis | Rejection letter/contract clauses plus vendor and buyer response |
| Canadian independence language reflects sovereignty concerns | Supported as challenge requirement | Award agreement, independent trust tool and test results |
| AUKUS interoperability trials show operational legal governors | Not established | Approved system configuration, safety case, legal review and change control |
| November 2026 Dstl projects already delivered | Chronologically false at cutoff | Future award/result records after the cutoff |

Evidence quality is strongest for policy scope, source identities and solicitation terms. Currentness varies: the transparency study is 2017, the maritime code 2022, UK directive 2024 and new competitions 2026. Implementation is observed as trials and research activity, but not independently validated weapon-safeguard outcomes. Funding figures here are availability/maximums; none is promoted as an outlay. Novelty is the documented gap between specific evidence requirements and public visibility of compliance, not discovery that safeguards never existed.

The strongest case leads are MOD's CARRION assurance-related switching rationale and Shield AI's parliamentary account of V-BAT approval/update friction. The latter is bounded by MAA guidance and ENAC's specific flight-authorisation record. The next reporting question is where an authority changed design, restricted deployment, demanded evidence or formally accepted residual risk. The cited records do not establish unlawful bypass or refusal to disclose model weights.
