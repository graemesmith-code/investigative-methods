# Five Eyes and AUKUS autonomy assurance and certification

Research memo prepared for F5 on 25 October 2026. Integrated from two reporting streams revised through three independent editor rounds and subsequent source-specific addenda. Public primary sources only unless expressly marked discovery lead. This is an evidence map for reporting, not a finding that all autonomy is unsafe or that published governance necessarily works.

## Finding and limits

The strongest named operational-permission friction case located is Shield AI's July 2026 evidence to Parliament: UK approval ambiguity, an informal risk-holder workaround, and burdensome update approvals. It is a named supplier's allegation, not an independently verified rejection. A maritime trade body's March 2026 submission reports months of delays and a six-figure rework bill, but conceals the affected members. Neither establishes a refusal to disclose model weights. Separately, October 2026 CARRION procurement notices explicitly cite the cost and schedule risk of recreating assurance evidence when justifying continued supplier dependence.

The strongest counterevidence is ENAC's public register of bounded V-BAT operating authorizations. An issuer also reports certification of BAE's autonomous naval boat; AIMS reports an AMSA testing exemption. Canadian trial rules establish conditions, but no named refusal was located. None of these proves effective autonomous weapons-release safeguards or approval of an entire AI stack.

UK policy expressly allows proportionate tailoring and compensating assurance. Its aviation regulator identifies conventional monitoring, overrides and operational restrictions as possible mitigations, while acknowledging adaptive AI is difficult to certify. This is materially different from either “no safeguards exist” or “opaque US models cannot certify.”

## Editorial choice after challenge

Lead with CARRION as a procurement-accountability story: MOD proposes continued noncompetitive supplier engagement and cites rebuilding assurance among its reasons. The benign explanation is sensible continuity for a bounded experimental phase, not permanent lock-in. The data-rights schedule and documented alternative-supplier assessment could materially change the interpretation. Do not lead with the late New Zealand acquisition approval as an “AI brake”: its acknowledged legislative barriers may apply equally to non-autonomous counter-drone equipment. That separate note is a boundary example, not evidence of an autonomy-specific rejection.

## Evidence grading

A = published primary decision, policy or formal rule. B = official programme/trial account, without independent performance evidence. C = named first-party allegation or certification-provider account. D = unnamed trade-body case, research recommendation or unverified discovery lead. A policy's existence is not an outcome; a test is not a legal weapons review; certification scope matters.

### Named regulatory friction for Shield AI V-BAT

**C — primary parliamentary evidence, not an adjudicated finding.** Shield AI's submission DEF0035, dated 27 July 2026, paragraphs 76–82, describes overlapping MAA/CAA responsibilities, an unclear approval route for V-BAT-sized NATO Class I aircraft, and an informal workaround involving a Delegated Duty Holder willing to accept the intended operational risk. It says navigating regulation consumed more time than operations and risk-assessment preparation. It also alleges software updates can prompt disproportionate reapproval; paragraph 86 proposes distinguishing minor from major changes.

The submission acknowledges MOD accommodation. It does not identify the operation, permit, DDH, elapsed delay, denied application, model version or evidence withheld. Do not recast it as a JSP 936 rejection or model-weight dispute.

**Important counterevidence:** RA 1600 Issue 10, dated 28 November 2025, establishes risk-based Open, Specific and Certified categories, with no weight ceiling for Specific S2 or Certified. Its scope depends on registration, ownership and operation; NATO Class I is not the controlling MAA category. A 10 May 2024 ministerial answer also confirms no-weight-limit Specific/Certified classes. The defensible allegation is an unclear route for Shield's particular operation, not an objectively absent Class I framework. Its registration and intended operation are essential to adjudication.

**Reporting asks:** obtain the initial classification, application and decision dates, DDH appointment, permission conditions, change categorisation and reapproval record; test the company's account against MAA/CAA records.

Sources: [Shield AI DEF0035](https://committees.parliament.uk/writtenevidence/168308/html/); [RA 1600 Issue 10](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/692958659c1eda2cdf034252/RA1600_Issue_10.pdf), pages 3 and 6–8; [ministerial answer 15576](https://questions-statements.parliament.uk/written-questions/detail/2024-02-26/15576/).

### Aviation assurance is not an absolute ban on opaque systems

**A — published regulator guidance, not itself a new mandatory regulation.** MAA/RN/2025/04, dated 10 October 2025, addresses AI in safety-related flight, propulsion and related systems. Certified-system applicants should raise a Military Certification Review Item for agreement. It favours fixed models within defined operating domains at the present stage of maturity and identifies adaptive safety-related AI as challenging. For some non-certified UAS, shortfalls can be addressed through safety-net architectures, manual overrides, kill switches and/or operational mitigations, proportionate to risk. The safety case must address residual uncertainty and monitor counterevidence.

The MAA index explicitly says Regulatory Notices do not constitute regulatory requirements. Obtain the actual MCRI, certification basis and accepted safety case before claiming these mitigations were demanded or implemented on a named platform.

Sources: [Current MAA/RN/2025/04 PDF](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/68f0b97c1c9076042263ef2a/MAA_RN_2025_04.pdf); [official notice index](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/maa-regulatory-notices-rn/).

### JSP 936 is a proportionate directive, not a public binary certification register

**A — JSP 936 Part 1 Directive, V1.1 November 2024**, published 13 November 2024. Its introductory material acknowledges an ideal end-state and unfinished tools. Paragraphs 17–18 permit tailoring where requirements cannot be met, with additional risk assessment and management. Paragraphs 198–201 demand confidence in externally supplied AI and allow additional MOD assurance to address gaps. The text does not impose a categorical public requirement to surrender all weights or training data.

Its foreword is signed by Alison Stevenson as Director General Delivery and Strategy. That is a historical document role, not a claim about her current appointment. Identify the live Senior Responsible Owner, accountable risk holder, tailored requirements and residual-risk acceptance for the actual programme.

Source: [JSP 936 Part 1](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6735fc89f6920bfb5abc7b62/JSP936_Part1.pdf).

### The Turing blocked-fielding vignette is fictitious

**D — September 2025 research report, not a disclosed rejection.** The Alan Turing Institute's “Defence AI Assurance: Identifying promising practice and a system card template for Defence” explicitly labels its page 8 scenario fictitious. A prototype that cannot be fielded because of certification/accountability issues is therefore not an investigative case. Its system-card template asks for full dataset access “if possible.” The PDF names the Defence AI Centre; the landing page's “Defence AI Research Centre” wording should not be propagated.

### System cards moved into MOD guidance in February 2026

**A — official guidance, not proof of completed deployment reviews.** DAIC's guidance, published 27 February 2026, broadly adopts the Turing template with changes, but makes that template optional. The SRO is responsible for keeping the card current. Teams should start when AI is selected or shortlisted, retain versions and record legal/contextual information. The guidance states there is currently no central MOD repository; teams store their cards internally.

This gives a useful records trail: original card, update history, approvals, changed context and inherited legal basis. It does not mean cards are absent, nor that no other central oversight exists.

Sources: [System-card guidance](https://digital.mod.uk/policy-rules-standards-and-guidance/aiph/development/guidance-on-system-cards); [documenting and storing cards](https://digital.mod.uk/policy-rules-standards-and-guidance/aiph/development/guidance-on-system-cards/documenting-storing-and-sharing-system-cards).

### Additional records of lifecycle implementation and its practical limits

The [January 2024 CETaS third-party assurance study](https://cetas.turing.ac.uk/publications/assurance-third-party-ai-systems-uk-national-security) identifies reduced direct oversight, supply-chain opacity and security/due-diligence risks when stages are outsourced. Its trial framework and hypothetical cases are research, not disclosed procurement disputes. The [February 2024 Defence AI Playbook](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/defence-artificial-intelligence-ai-playbook) supplies use-case leads; the [10 June 2026 leadership letter](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/putting-artificial-intelligence-ai-at-the-heart-of-uk-defence/putting-artificial-intelligence-ai-at-the-heart-of-uk-defence) demonstrates pressure for faster adoption and workforce skills. Neither establishes a safety waiver.

The [DAIC Assurance Framework](https://digital.mod.uk/policy-rules-standards-and-guidance/aiph/the-defence-ai-centre-ai-assurance-framework) and [responsibility guidance](https://www.digital.mod.uk/policy-rules-standards-and-guidance/aiph/responsibility-for-assuring-ai-in-defence), dated 27 February 2026, cover people, process, evidence and technology. MOD owns JSP compliance; suppliers need assurance processes; RAISOs oversee organisational adoption and SROs own programme risks. The [model-card guidance](https://www.digital.mod.uk/policy-rules-standards-and-guidance/aiph/development/guidance-on-model-cards) explains why model cards alone are insufficient. These are public implementation processes, not audited compliance rates.

The [deployment sign-off guidance](https://www.digital.mod.uk/policy-rules-standards-and-guidance/aiph/assurance/step-by-step-guide-to-assuring-ai/signing-off-and-deploying-an-ai-system) expects tested mitigations, trained users and SRO risk satisfaction. Its [live-system monitoring page](https://www.digital.mod.uk/policy-rules-standards-and-guidance/aiph/assurance/step-by-step-guide-to-assuring-ai/monitoring-a-live-ai-system) identifies data/context/model drift and new operators as risks, requiring risk-register updates, mitigation and escalation when pausing or change is needed. Significant changes may require reapproval. This assurance reapproval must not be equated automatically with a fresh legal weapons review. Optional templates and decentralised storage establish neither lost records nor failed controls.

The DAIC-commissioned [May 2026 Resilient Defence AI report](https://www.turing.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2026-05/the_alan_turing_institute_daic_-_resilient_defence_ai_report.pdf) adds power, cooling, communications and supply-chain context; printed pp.35–36 explain how interfaces may obscure degraded conditions. Annex B proposes sustainability additions to cards. This is research/proposed practice, not a revised mandatory standard or named certification failure. The public PDF was retrieved/read locally; it is cited rather than rehosted.

### Human-factors evidence and assurance capacity

Published winners include a proprietary-AI offering, countering any automatic inference that proprietary systems cannot enter government trials.
