# Enforcement afterlife and monitoring gaps, 2016–2026

Research date: 13 October 2026. These are investigation designs, not new allegations. Ranking is editorial judgment. Public source availability is partly verified at the portal/document level; no entity-level pilot or cost benchmark has been run. No outreach, paid calls, secret financial data, or allegations based only on a mismatch.

## Recommendation

Three directions survive the “would a document comparison already do this?” test: follow businesses after exclusion; follow mines after suspension/control changes; enumerate the professional-service activity that supervision registers fail to describe. The novelty is **persistent, branching case research over events and legal entities**, not OCR, red-flag arithmetic, or a new graph UI. None is AI-exclusive. What changes is the cost of maintaining hundreds of small, defensible dossiers as names, subsidiaries, permissions, projects, and source locations change.

## 1. Did the ban stop the business, or just the named company?

**Question:** After a contractor is excluded, do the same business operations continue winning covered contracts through successors, affiliates, joint ventures or subcontracting, and did the applicable exclusion actually extend to that recipient?

**Why consequential:** Tests whether a central anti-corruption deterrent has practical effect. Outcomes could be contract exposure and an accountability gap, even when no criminal evasion can be proved.

**NGO basis:** Spotlight’s March 2021 procurement consultation, Annex 1 section 5 (PDF p12; also Q22 p9), warns that fragmented qualification/performance records let companies or directors escape detection under new company names. Its Power Without Responsibility (February 2024), printed pp55–58, identifies weak monitoring of disqualified directors’ indirect influence. This motivates longitudinal research, rather than assuming it establishes current misconduct.

**Cohort without famous names:** All World Bank debarments issued in a fixed 12-month window; select a geography only after scoring public company/procurement access. Alternatively, all UK CMA cartel decisions or director disqualifications in that window. Do not choose the most scandalous names first. Stratify by sanction period, sector and corporate complexity; report attrition from inaccessible records.

**Agent loop:**
1. Read the sanction’s operative text: exact entity, identifiers, affiliates, start/end dates, release conditions, project/geographic scope and appeals.
2. Build a dated pre-sanction identity snapshot from registry and official decisions. Separate company identity, brand, business assets and individuals.
3. Watch new awards, contract modifications, insolvencies, acquisitions, new subsidiaries and renamed websites. A trigger generates a *new search plan*, not a match score alone.
4. Follow evidence of continuity: business-transfer announcement → acquirer filing → assets/brand/employees/contracts acquired → successor’s procurement identifiers → new award date and applicable project rules.
5. Search deliberately for release, self-cleaning, court permission, stays, exceptions and genuinely independent ownership. Preserve negative findings and sources searched.
6. Produce a short event dossier with every edge tied to a source passage, and a separate legal-scope checklist. Human review precedes allegation or outreach.

**Verified public starting points:**
- World Bank ineligible entities: https://www.worldbank.org/en/Projects-operations/procurement/debarred-firms . The list explicitly distinguishes controlled/controlling affiliates and successors/assigns in post-2021 grounds, and contains cross-debarment limitations. Its corporate-procurement list includes a 2023 three-entity case whose stated grounds concern concealed ownership/beneficial interest: https://www.worldbank.org/en/about/corporate-procurement/business-opportunities/non-responsible-vendors . These are evidence that the problem is real, not proof of later evasion.
- UK current scope and exceptions: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/procurement-act-2023-guidance-documents-procure-phase/guidance-debarment-html . Connected/associated persons and subcontractors matter; mandatory and discretionary grounds differ. Procurement Act regime began February 2025, so do not apply it retroactively to every award.
- Director register and permissions: https://www.gov.uk/search-the-register-of-disqualified-company-directors ; https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/corporate-insolvency-effect-of-a-disqualification-order/effect-of-a-disqualification . Continued employment is not automatically banned management; permission may exist.

**Old baseline:** Sanctions-list/award joins and corporate graphs are established. Open Ownership’s September 2023 BODS proof of concept already combined BO, procurement and sanctions. The additional product must reconstruct business continuity outside structured ownership records, interpret case-specific scope, and keep checking until the sanction expires or an award is adjudicated.

**Falsifiers/innocent explanations:** Unrelated same-name company; passive shared office/agent; bona fide acquisition by an independent purchaser; lawful employment; court permission; expired sanction; eligible non-covered contract; approved self-cleaning; disclosure/award lags. A missing entry is not evasion.

**Cheap pilot and stop gate:** Start with 30–50 sanctions, free official decisions and company pages, one public registry jurisdiction. Record minutes/tokens per resolved entity and how many have usable award data. If most successors or subcontractors cannot be observed without paid registries or non-public procurement files, reject that jurisdiction. Human legal verification is the binding expense. Do not promise automated proof of covert control.

## 2. The mine that “closed”: what rights and operations survived the enforcement announcement?

**Question:** Following a permit suspension, revocation, sale or owner change, what happens to the actual project, its economic beneficiaries and the obligations attached to it? Is there a lawful reauthorisation, a new permit/vehicle, retained royalty interest, or merely a headline without a traceable public resolution?

**NGO basis:** NRGI’s 2018 beneficial-ownership licensing briefing found that about half of over 50 reviewed laws prohibited certain official interests, but none required the licensing screen to check those interests. Its 2024 Ten Red Flags, printed pp26–27, treats intermediaries and residual benefits as investigation leads; pp32–33 covers consequences for environmental/social obligations. Most importantly, the August 2026 Indonesia RGA identifies fragmented post-award information on inspections, sanctions, amendments and ownership. Its companion article discusses renewed licensing activity around Raja Ampat after 2025 revocations, and PT Gag Nikel resuming operations; these are reported context, not evidence of illegality.

**Cohort without famous names:** A dated census of all publicly announced permit suspensions/revocations in a chosen mineral/jurisdiction, plus all publicly disclosed mine acquisitions during the same period. Project/permit ID, not company name, is the unit. Start where permit polygons and legal decisions are public; do not start with an opaque country merely because the stakes are large.

**Agent loop:**
1. Read the operative order, its conditions and appeal route. Record concession coordinates and stable project identifiers.
2. Follow subsequent cadastre/gazette events and listed-company announcements. Resolve renamed projects, split concessions, overlapping licence areas and changes in operating company.
3. When a transfer appears, recursively retrieve transaction terms and later completion notices: equity sold versus retained royalties, offtake, options, debt security and management rights. “No longer owns shares” does not mean “no longer benefits.”
4. Find the approval or reinstatement act. Check whether obligations attach to the licence/project, former operator, new company or guarantor.
5. Where free imagery is suitable, use it only to triage material physical change. A new excavation or shipment report triggers documentary review; imagery alone cannot establish authorised operating status or pollutant causation.
6. Build the project’s dated lifecycle: enforcement → appeal/transfer → permission → observed operation → unresolved obligation. Report inability to trace the public decision separately from an asserted breach.

**Public evidence paths:** Indonesia MOMI/MODI and EITI portals are linked by NRGI; their project-level accessibility still needs a pilot. ResourceContracts offers searchable PDFs/text/metadata and warns about missing annexes/OCR errors: https://www.resourcecontracts.org/faqs?lang=en . The current inventory can select a non-famous cohort: https://www.resourcecontracts.org/contracts?lang=en . ASX publishes current/historical company announcements, with historic personal use free per its FAQ; automated republication/licensing must be checked: https://www.asx.com.au/asx/v2/statistics/announcements.do and https://www.asx.com.au/investors/investment-tools-and-resources/faq . Issuers’ own disclosure archives are another source.

**Why agents:** Each event changes what the next relevant document is: an acquisition can make a financing footnote or royalty schedule more important than the original licence. A batch clause comparer does not discover the undisclosed-to-the-portal annex, successor, approving ministry decision or next filing. Autonomous retrieval can maintain the investigatory thread across languages and corporate events, while humans judge law and public interest.

**Old baseline:** ResourceContracts had searchable scans, text, summaries and comparison tools by 2016; NRGI published 12 red flags in 2017. Satellite mine-change detection is also not new. The advance must be continuous legal/project/economic lineage with source discovery, not “AI reads mining contracts.”

**Falsifiers:** Lawful reinstatement; distinct neighbouring concession; exploration allowed while production suspended; care-and-maintenance earthworks; completed independent sale; valid consent; legally retained disclosed royalty; historic imagery. Unavailable approval is an opacity story unless absence can independently be established.

**Cheap pilot/kill:** Twenty projects; one commodity; one jurisdiction with freely readable orders, cadastre and issuer filings. Reject any promised analysis of taxes paid when those payments are confidential. Indonesia is especially consequential but NRGI scores contract access zero; that can make it a bad *cheap first pilot*. Choose another accessible country if the necessary evidence is missing. No paid imagery is necessary for initial triage; human geospatial/legal interpretation remains necessary.

**Key sources:**
- https://resourcegovernance.org/publications/beneficial-ownership-screening-practical-measures-reduce-corruption-risks-extractives
- https://resourcegovernance.org/sites/default/files/2024-12/Ten_Red_Flags_Corruption_Risk_Transition_Minerals.pdf
- https://resourcegovernance.org/articles/what-happens-after-mining-license-granted-indonesia-first-rga

## 3. The missing denominator: who is selling high-risk corporate services outside visible supervision?

**Question:** Among publicly active company-formation, registered-office, nominee/trust, conveyancing and other in-scope service providers, how many can be assigned to a valid AML supervisor for the service and period in question? How do disciplined providers’ services persist through a new entity or channel?

**Why not a directory comparison:** The denominator is the hard work. Business registers classify companies badly, regulated service categories depend on actual activity, and brands/groups use multiple entities. Agents can discover and classify concrete offers, then pursue the appropriate legal entity and regulator through several documents.

**NGO basis:** Spotlight’s Broken Record (March 2025), printed pp25–29, diagnoses self-declared regulatory scope, trust/company-service risks and fragmented supervision. GFI’s 2021 Acres of Money Laundering uses case research to identify lawyers/other gatekeepers as meaningful facilitators, but its US-law claims must not be repeated as current UK law. TJN’s 2023 BO critique explains why ownership-register thresholds alone will not supply a complete picture of control/economic exposure.

**Cohort:** All active companies in a fixed set of company-service/accountancy/legal SIC categories and a fixed geography, supplemented by systematically sampled public service advertisements/search categories. Include a random low-risk control set and explicitly measure SIC/search coverage bias. Alternatively seed with all published AML penalties in one year and follow each business’s service continuation. Do not start from names of oligarch lawyers.

**Agent loop:**
1. Discover service pages; classify actual offers against a lawyer-reviewed regulatory rubric, with evidence excerpts.
2. Resolve trading brand → contracting entity via terms, footer, Companies House and professional register. Record service type and dates.
3. Check *all relevant* supervisors, not just HMRC. Resolve group/umbrella registrations, dual supervision, exemptions and stale records.
4. On a cancellation or penalty, inspect whether the same offer moves to another entity, address, domain or professional body. Shared infrastructure is a lead, not proof.
5. Recheck unresolved cases after an appropriate registration/publication lag; output confident positives, explainable gaps and unresolved cases separately.
6. Prepare case-specific questions for a journalist to put to regulator/provider. No autonomous accusation or regulatory complaint.

**Public starting points:** HMRC’s full downloadable supervised register provides registration/trading names, sectors and supervision date: https://www.gov.uk/guidance/money-laundering-regulations-supervised-business-register . HMRC explicitly warns about publication delays, alternate group names and pending registrations. SRA’s professional register is useful but not equivalent to a comprehensive activity-specific AML register. HMRC’s dual-supervision handbook distinguishes FCA authorisation from AML registration: https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/economic-crime-supervision-handbook/ecsh42000 .

**Freshness caveat:** A consolidated FCA system is planned, **not already implemented**. FCA’s 22 September 2026 page says existing arrangements continue, phased transfer is expected from late 2028 with remaining professional bodies by 2030: https://www.fca.org.uk/firms/aml-supervisory-reform . The current pitch is establish the missing denominator and a baseline for handover, not announce present-day transition failures.

**Falsifiers:** Provider only markets but does not perform service; authorised subcontractor delivers it; parent/group registration; different supervisor; exempt activity; overseas jurisdiction; uncompleted registration; lag; old website; dissolved brand; outdated sanctions. Non-registration does not establish laundering or intent.

**Cheap pilot/kill:** A 100-provider local/service sample, with high-confidence activity classification on 20. Reusable free register downloads keep lookup costs small. If affiliation/supervision cannot be resolved publicly, report an observability rate rather than inflate a rogue-provider count. Publication needs a regulated-sector specialist and right of reply; cheap lead generation does not eliminate editorial/legal cost.

## Reserve ideas rejected or downgraded

- **Sherpa duty-of-vigilance perimeter:** Strong problem, weaker fit. The 2021 Radar identified 44 apparent non-publishers and said an exhaustive population was impossible amid group opacity. Rebuilding headcount/group thresholds is useful but too close to the banal comparison the user rejected unless tied to a concrete restructuring that shifts accountability after a harm. Keep only as a branch of an actual case.
- **BO threshold puzzles:** TJN correctly raises share thresholds, control and verification. Automated ownership arithmetic/registry contradictions alone is already established and often cannot overcome secret trusts. Only promote when a public transaction reveals meaningful control/economic rights outside the apparent ownership graph.
- **Recovered-assets attrition:** Basel’s 2023 recommendations 25–29 call for asset registries, timely decisions/return agreements and transparency across stages. Valuable question, but public evidence may end at a freeze or return press release. Do not pitch an end-to-end money trail without a country-specific proof that auction, transfer, budget and expenditure records can be linked. Basel Case Study 10 (2023) shows why freeze/confiscation/return are legally distinct, especially when war impairs mutual legal assistance. This is a reserve, not a cheap finalist.
- **GFI trade-value gaps:** Not a fraud detector. Reject if it treats bilateral discrepancies as corrupt transfers or requires confidential customs/bank data to validate the central claim.

## Common evaluation rule

For each candidate measure: fraction of cohort with resolvable identifiers; fraction with two independent public sources for consequential linkage; research minutes per resolved case; false-positive reasons; unseen-document discovery over a deterministic join baseline; number of meaningful changes caught on repeat checks. A good system should close many leads as innocent. Stop a pilot if it only produces name matches, inaccessible-data demands or open-ended insinuations.
