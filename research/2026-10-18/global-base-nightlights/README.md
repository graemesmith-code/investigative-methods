# The changing geography of U.S. military sites, seen at night
## NGO research proposal | 18 October 2026 | Version 0.1

**Funding recommendation:** commission a six-week feasibility study, budgeted at **US$32,775**, before committing to a global atlas. A conditional expansion is budgeted at **US$135,700 additional**, for a combined planning envelope of **US$168,475** over approximately five to seven months. These are transparent staffing assumptions, not supplier quotations. The expansion's coverage must be renegotiated against measured inventory and boundary-verification costs.

**Research question:** How has nighttime illumination changed at publicly documented U.S. military sites since 2012, and how do those changes relate to independently documented openings, expansions, departures, transfers and physical closures?

**Proposed public product:** an evidence-linked historical atlas and downloadable research dataset, connecting the documented history of sites with their measured illumination. It would show where sustained physical changes deserve reporting and where satellite observations cannot resolve the question.

**Status:** research design and source feasibility checks completed; global satellite extraction, historical inventory reconstruction and validation have not been performed. This proposal makes no finding that the U.S. military has globally contracted, expanded or reshored.

### 1. Why this deserves a pilot

A historical study asks a more plausible question of nighttime satellite data than the earlier attempt to detect overnight activity at the Pentagon. Large, sustained changes in illuminated facilities may be observable over months and years. Small staffing changes inside occupied buildings may produce little detectable external light.

The public-interest opportunity is to connect the physical landscape with accounts of basing policy: departure versus transfer, consolidation versus expansion, and military land converted to civilian use. Potential stories include facilities that remain illuminated after U.S. departure, expansion outside the former war zones, and the local aftermath of closures. Findings may complicate the proposed retrenchment narrative.

Nightlights cannot establish troop numbers, expenditure, weapons capacity, operational readiness, electricity consumption or which military operates a shared facility. A dimming site is a reporting lead, not proof of withdrawal. A map of U.S. departures requires documentary evidence whether or not the lights change.

### 2. What we borrow from the World Bank—and change

The November 2024 World Bank working paper *Afghanistan’s New Economic Landscape* separates military and civilian illumination using spatial masks. Its analysis combines annual VIIRS-like imagery and monthly VIIRS composites; it is not a NASA Black Marble replication template. Its military-site discovery includes light variability followed by visual checks, and it acknowledges difficulty separating military and civilian light in dense areas. [S1]

We retain the spatial separation and historical perspective. Our adaptation will select sites and date changes independently of nightlights, preventing selection on the outcome being measured. The authors’ public repository also describes brightness-derived status classifications; those cannot serve as independent labels for testing whether light reveals U.S. presence. [S2]

This is a methodological extension requiring validation across different settings. We will first reproduce an available published Afghanistan result with the authors’ inputs, or explicitly label any exercise using different inputs a conceptual reproduction. We will not present the paper’s macroeconomic validation as proof that global base illumination measures military activity.

### 3. What the study would measure

| Layer | Measurement | Permitted interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Documented presence | Site status and dated evidence of U.S. use | Publicly documented geographic presence, with gaps |
| Physical footprint | Verified site polygons and documented changes in area | Land/facility extent, where boundaries are known |
| Illumination | Quality-screened radiance inside a fixed geographic footprint | Satellite-observed light at that location |
| Event comparison | Illumination before and after documented changes | Association with an event; causality requires additional evidence |
| Reporting cases | Records, imagery and local reporting together | Explanations of individual transformations |

The headline distinction is **“light at places used by the U.S. military” versus “light generated by the U.S. military.”** Often only the first is observable.

We propose January 2012–December 2025 as the intended monthly window, conditional on a complete tile-by-month availability audit. NASA lists its monthly Black Marble Collection 2 product from January 2012. This does not guarantee usable observations at every site or a complete first year. Treat 2012 as an inception year until checked; use 2013–2015 as a candidate reference period only where coverage supports it. Keep partial 2026 separate. [S3]

### 4. The inventory must include former sites

The FY2024 geocoded inventory is a starting register, not the historical study population. Our inspection found 1,624 coordinate-bearing records, but only 1,546 distinct exact coordinate pairs; neither count establishes independent bases. Some coordinates identify towns or parent facilities. The list includes domestic sites. [S4]

Starting only with 2024 sites would omit some places abandoned earlier—the very change the research seeks to describe. We therefore need the union of sites documented during 2012–2025, assembled from historical Base Structure Reports, the Vine historical lists, official U.S. and host-country records, and documented corrections. The inventory should record unknown status instead of treating disappearance from a list as closure. [S5–S7]

Show the 50 states and Washington, D.C., U.S. territories, and foreign countries separately. Definitions must remain stable over time. An installation may contain several sites; an access agreement may provide no separately identifiable U.S. footprint. Shared airports, small leased offices and uncertain pins will often be unsuitable for site-level nightlight attribution.

The official NTAD polygon service queried during scoping returned only the country value “usa.” It is not a solution to overseas boundary coverage. Overseas site geometry is a substantial research task. [S8]

### 5. Feasibility study: six weeks, 48 sites

Build a stratified validation set before inspecting its light trends:

- 12 documented physical openings or substantial expansions;
- 12 documented physical closures/decommissions;
- 12 U.S. departures or transfers where subsequent use is independently established;
- 12 continuing facilities without a documented major change in the chosen comparison window.

Aim for representation across at least four broad regions, including cases outside Afghanistan and Iraq, with isolated, urban/shared, cloudy and snowy settings. This is a deliberately varied diagnostic sample, not a statistically representative global sample. Class membership and event dates require records. Failure to document a change is not evidence that nothing changed.

Use 24 sites for development and 24 held out for validation, six of each event class in each group. Keep geographically linked installations and the same withdrawal episode together where feasible; record any remaining dependence. If credible cases cannot be assembled, reduce the scope rather than invent ground truth. No candidate is included because it has a striking light curve.

Test three distinct propositions:

1. **Measurement:** can the method recover sustained changes at independently documented physical transformations?
2. **Interpretation:** does that information distinguish a U.S. departure from continued use by another operator?
3. **Utility:** does it yield verifiable reporting leads beyond what the records alone reveal?

Expected outputs: a source-audited pilot register, reproducible extraction code, complete quality accounting, a held-out results table including failures, six to eight example case files selected across outcomes, and an independently reviewed expansion recommendation.

### 6. Technical approach

Use monthly NASA Black Marble Collection 2 as the primary candidate; compare a small subset with an independent processing product. Preserve one satellite/platform series where possible and never silently splice sensors or collections. Freeze input versions.

Extract polygons rather than a single GPS pixel. Maintain a fixed reference footprint for the time series and separately record dated boundary changes. Use matched civilian comparison areas to diagnose regional changes, without assuming that subtraction perfectly isolates military activity.

Keep observed high-quality data separate from historical gap filling. NASA’s monthly quality definitions differ from the daily flags used in our earlier pilot. The technical protocol specifies product checks, provisional coverage gates, sensitivity tests and treatment of snow, clouds, urban contamination and missing observations.

Report both physical radiance and a normalized within-site change. Do not allow an attractive animation to imply uniform data quality or continuous coverage. All global summaries must disclose the sites and area included; publish results with and without Afghanistan and Iraq.

### 7. Funding gates

**Advance to broader measurement only if:**

- independent reviewers can reproduce the principal pilot outputs;
- usable longitudinal measurements cover at least 70% of the planned pilot sites overall, with coverage reported by setting;
- measurable physical-change cases show a consistent signal in the held-out sample that survives reasonable geometry, quality and background choices;
- the per-site verification cost supports a clearly specified expansion sample;
- adequate sites outside Afghanistan and Iraq support any claim of transferability.

The 70% gate is a proposed management threshold, not a scientific constant. The detailed protocol sets provisional signal rules and reports uncertainty rather than turning 24 held-out sites into a global accuracy claim.

**Proceed with a narrower atlas** if illumination works mainly for large isolated facilities. Label eligible and ineligible sites transparently.

**Abandon the withdrawal-proxy claim** if transfers frequently leave lights unchanged or brighten them. The documented-presence atlas and physical-change cases can still be valuable.

**Stop global nightlight expansion** if light changes are dominated by geometry, background contamination, lighting technology or missingness. Publish a short negative-methods report and retain the independently documented historical register.

No funding gate requires the world to exhibit a net decline.

**Pilot schedule:** week 1 source/rights review, case sampling and availability audit; weeks 2–3 geometry/history verification and development extraction; week 4 freeze protocol and run held-out cases; week 5 sensitivity and independent review; week 6 findings, reporting utility assessment and priced expansion plan. Parallel staffing is required; 40 person-days do not mean 40 elapsed workdays.

**Conditional expansion:** approximately 16–22 additional weeks. Register all discovered candidate sites and process all eligible verified geometries. The budget is a capacity allowance, not a promise to validate all 1,624 pins or an unknown larger historical union. At one hour of historical/boundary work per site, 1,624 sites alone require about 232 seven-hour researcher-days before review. Existing reliable polygons can reduce this greatly; ambiguous overseas sites can increase it. Use pilot timings to agree a count and regional distribution before awarding expansion funding.

An affordable first deliverable can be a global register with a transparently selected subset of reliable light series. A comprehensive all-site atlas requires a separately costed coverage commitment.

### 9. Deliverables and editorial value

The expansion would deliver:

1. A versioned historical register with aliases, source links, occupancy intervals, geometry provenance and explicit uncertainty.
2. Monthly site-level radiance and quality tables, with a reproducible annual/seasonal summary.
3. A historical atlas separating operator/status from light, including gray missing-data states and evidence-linked case pages.
4. A methods and validation report, plus a documented list of exclusions.
5. Several independently reported stories: where departures coincide with physical contraction, where sites remain in use, and where investment or expansion occurred elsewhere.
6. Code, processing manifests and reusable public-data outputs where licensing permits.

Connections to the future-of-warfare inquiry should be tested through independent facility, construction and procurement evidence. Brightening cannot identify AI investment, autonomous weapons or a transfer of military assets back to the United States. “Reshoring” requires records linking particular activities or assets between locations; simultaneous domestic brightening and overseas dimming is insufficient.

**Suggested NGO pitch:** “We propose a reproducible historical atlas of documented U.S. military sites from 2012 onward, combining records of who used each site with satellite measurements of its illumination. A six-week feasibility study will establish where this measurement is reliable, where it fails, and whether a larger investigation offers enough public-interest value to justify its cost.”
