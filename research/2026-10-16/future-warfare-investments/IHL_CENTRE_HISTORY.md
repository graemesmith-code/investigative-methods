# Civilian Protection Center of Excellence: identity and history

**Initial history note, 16 October 2026.** The [current team-and-product deep dive](track-2-ihl/TEAM_CONTINUITY.md) adds a directly readable 13 October congressional source for the reported seven-person staffing level and follows named people and products. This first-pass note is preserved; newer evidence and unresolved continuity questions are set out in [Track 2](track-2-ihl/README.md).

Research checked 16 October 2026.

## Bottom line

The likely institution referenced is the U.S. Department of Defense's **Civilian Protection Center of Excellence (CP CoE/CPCoE)**. The U.S. context, documented technology work and subsequent severe reduction make it a strong match. Searches did not establish a different centre fitting the description; the identification remains provisional.

Two qualifications matter:

- Its documented remit was civilian-harm mitigation and response, including better information and decision support. This review identified **no primary evidence that its programme literally encoded IHL into targeting algorithms**, or automated the legal determination that a strike was lawful.
- It was severely reduced, but **“it no longer does that” is too absolute**. The FY2025 report, as described by later reporting and legal analysis, records a Maven civilian-environment layer and an AskSage-based assistant. Their exact current operational use, effectiveness and AI-specific expenditure remain unverified.

## Verified history and sources

1. **25 August 2022: policy origin.** The CHMR Action Plan called for a centre to coordinate analysis, learning and training, alongside better civilian-environment information, reporting, data management and operational practices. This is a broader institutional mission than software encoding of law.
   - DoD fact sheet: https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/article/3140007/civilian-harm-mitigation-and-response-action-plan-fact-sheet/

2. **23 December 2022: statutory mandate.** Section 1082 of the FY2023 NDAA added 10 U.S.C. §184. It requires the Secretary to operate the centre, improve civilian-harm practices and tools, maintain reporting/data processes and learn from incidents. The text current through 7 October 2026 still contains that requirement.
   - Official U.S. Code: https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/119-118/t10/s184/

3. **March 2023: establishment.** The Army's FY2023 historical summary dates headquarters' establishment of the centre to March 2023. It was an Army organisation with a department-wide mission.
   - Army history: https://history.army.mil/portals/143/Images/Publications/catalog/101-54-1.pdf
   - Appointment of Michael McNerney as director announced 17 April 2023: https://www.defense.gov/News/Transcripts/Transcript/Article/3365469/deputy-pentagon-press-secretary-sabrina-singh-holds-a-press-briefing/

4. **FY2024: actual technology activity.** Its annual report says two contracted technology analysts investigated emerging technology, including AI, for CHMR gaps and supported testing/evaluation in experiments and exercises. It also supported the Army's civilian-harm data platform and analytical methods. That establishes resourced AI-related work, not a legal-reasoning or executable-IHL programme.
   - Official report, search-index text retrieved; direct document fetch failed: https://policy.defense.gov/Portals/11/Documents/CHMR/CP_CoE_FY2024_Report-508-Compliant.pdf?ver=MbjJrGoKDeoOGU28JJ0gPw%3D%3D

5. **2025–April 2026: severe reduction without completed formal abolition.** The May 2026 Inspector General report states that February 2025 proposals contemplated elimination/significant reduction; a 29 May 2025 legislative proposal sought repeal of §184. As of April 2026 no formal decision had been announced, but CHMR implementation had stalled/reversed, personnel had left and Army funding for the data platform had stopped. The report distinguishes these facts from the continuing statutory duties.
   - Official IG report, 14 May 2026, relevant text indexed at report page 12: https://media.defense.gov/2026/May/14/2003930527/-1/-1/1/DOWIG-2026-084_REDACTED%20SECURE.PDF

6. **FY2025 report: residual AI work.** Sarah Shoker and Jessica Dorsey's 17 August 2026 article links the department's FY2025 annual report and describes a Maven Smart System civilian-environment layer and an assistant using AskSage, with a claimed 97% reduction in assessment time during one Army exercise. They stress that the performance claim is not independently verified. The article records staffing falling from about 40 in January 2025 to nine at year end. Its phrase “approximately two-thirds” is arithmetically inconsistent with those numbers; use the raw counts instead.
   - Analysis with primary-report link: https://opiniojuris.org/2026/08/17/testing-the-limits-ai-enabled-targeting-model-evaluation-and-international-humanitarian-law/
   - Primary FY2025 report URL verified by following that link, but PDF returned HTTP 403 to web and urllib, so its content was **not directly inspected**: https://policy.war.gov/Portals/11/Documents/CHMR/TAB%20B%20-%20DoW%202025%20CP%20CoE%20Annual%20Report%206.15.26-508.pdf?ver=tEr0PWd4eLNHXneLp3UNUA%3D%3D

## Funding context, not AI budget totals

- The **FY2024 Army budget request** added $16.300 million and 40 FTE for Army CHMR implementation, including 30 centre staff, four data-platform staff and other coordination roles. This is not an AI-specific allocation or verified outlay.

## Interpretation and uncertainties

Civilian-environment data, no-strike-list support and harm estimation can help humans apply IHL. That does not establish executable legal rules, autonomous proportionality judgments or legally reliable targeting. Generic responsible-AI testing and weapons legal review are further distinct activities. Avoid representing all such expenditure as spending on “putting IHL into algorithms.”

Former centre law/policy adviser Chris Rogers argued on **23 March 2026** that no DoD actor appeared institutionally tasked with directing AI investment toward IHL/civilian protection and that dedicated programmes/resources were lacking. This is useful informed testimony, not proof of zero spending; the subsequently published FY2025 activity descriptions make an unqualified zero-spending conclusion especially unsafe.
